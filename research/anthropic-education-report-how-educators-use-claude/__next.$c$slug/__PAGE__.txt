post-text">Understandably, much of the conversation of AI in education focuses on how students are using large language models to help them study and write. But educators use AI too. In a recent Gallup survey, teachers reported that AI tools saved them an average of 5.9 hours per week. And in an inversion of the usual discussion, students have begun expressing concerns about <em>professors</em> using AI in the classroom.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We previously <a href="/news/anthropic-education-report-how-university-students-use-claude">reported data</a> on how students were using AI. Our new analysis looks at professors: we analyzed ~74,000 anonymized conversations from higher education professionals across the world on Claude.ai this past May and June.<sup class="caption PA4F1q_sup PA4F1q_footnote-marker"><a href="#footnote-1" id="footnote-ref-1" class="PA4F1q_footnote-ref" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup> We also partnered with Northeastern University to hear directly from faculty how they were using AI within the university. Our findings provide an empirical snapshot of educator AI adoption, specifically in university settings.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="table"><div class="Z3bHXa_wrapper"><div role="presentation" style="position:relative;--scroll-area-corner-height:0px;--scroll-area-corner-width:0px" class="Y0gYHq_root"><div role="presentation" data-id="base-ui-_R_23drpbsnpfkq6btb_-viewport" tabindex="-1" class="Y0gYHq_viewport base-ui-disable-scrollbar" style="overflow: scroll; they're building their own custom tools with AI <br></strong>Faculty are using <a href="/news/build-artifacts">Claude Artifacts</a> to create interactive educational materials, such as chemistry simulations, automated grading rubrics, and data visualization dashboards.</p></div></div></div><div class="Z3bHXa_row" role="row"><div class="tOQyXa_body-4 Z3bHXa_cell" data-component="Body" role="cell"><div class="RMuY0W_root YQKUZG_wrapper" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><p><strong>Educators tend to automate the drudgery while staying in the loop for everything else</strong><br>Tasks requiring significant context, creativity, or direct student interaction—like designing lessons, advising students, and writing grant proposals—are where educators are more likely to use AI as an enhancement. In contrast, routine administrative work such as financial management and record-keeping are more automation-heavy.</p></div></div></div><div class="Z3bHXa_row" role="row"><div class="tOQyXa_body-4 Z3bHXa_cell" data-component="Body" role="cell"><div class="RMuY0W_root YQKUZG_wrapper" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><p><strong>Some educators are automating grading; others are deeply opposed</strong><br>In our Claude.ai data, faculty used AI for grading and evaluation less frequently than other uses, but when they did, 48.9% of the time they used it in an automation-heavy way (where the AI directly performs the task). That’s despite educator concerns about automating assessment tasks, as well as our surveyed faculty rating it as the area where they felt AI was least effective.</p></div></div></div></div></div></div></div></div></div></div><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="identifying-educators-use-of-claude">Identifying educators’ use of Claude</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">In this research, we used <a href="/research/clio">our automated analysis research tool</a> that reveals broad patterns of Claude usage while protecting users’ privacy.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Studying higher education professionals’ use of Claude.ai presents unique challenges, as we don’t currently collect self-reported occupational data on our platform. Unlike students who often explicitly mention coursework or assignments, educators’ AI interactions span teaching, research, administration, and personal learning, making them harder to identify and categorize.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Using our privacy-preserving tool, we analyzed conversations from Claude.ai Free and Pro accounts associated with higher education email addresses and then automatically filtered conversations for educator-specific tasks—such as creating syllabi, grading assignments, or developing course materials.<sup class="caption PA4F1q_sup PA4F1q_footnote-marker"><a href="#footnote-2" id="footnote-ref-2" class="PA4F1q_footnote-ref" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup> This filtering yielded approximately 74,000 conversations from a period in May and June. Our analysis should be viewed as an exploration of how educators use AI for profession-specific tasks, not a comprehensive view of all educator AI usage.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We also matched each conversation to the most appropriate task from the comprehensive list of educator tasks in the O*NET database of occupational information from the U.S. Department of Labor. We identified educator tasks as tasks associated with “Postsecondary” teaching or administrative occupations.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We complemented our analysis with survey data and qualitative research from 22 Northeastern University faculty members who are early adopters of AI to shed light on educators' motivations, concerns, and usage patterns.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="common-uses-among-educators">Common uses among educators</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">The most prominent use of AI, as revealed by both our Claude.ai analysis and our qualitative research with Northeastern, was for curriculum development. Our Claude.ai analysis also surfaced academic research and assessing student performance as the second and third most common uses.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="Bar chart showing three educational AI use cases: &quot;Develop curricula&quot; (57%), &quot;Conduct academic research&quot; (13%), and &quot;Assess student performance&quot; (7%). Each category shows common requests and the split between augmentation and automation approaches, with augmentation generally preferred over automation across all categories." loading="lazy" width="2880" height="858" decoding="async" data-nimg="1" style="color:transparent" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/4d71f1145f71f4acbeed.png"><figcaption class="RMuY0W_root -LJkNq_container -LJkNq_container--default" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><div class="tOQyXa_caption-1 -LJkNq_text" data-component="Caption">Top three AI uses among educators, as based on 74,000 conversations of Claude.ai data: Develop curricula (57% of the conversations in our analysis), Conduct academic research (13%), and Assess student performance (7%). The augmentation/automation spectrum of how faculty use AI for these tasks is also displayed.</div></figcaption></figure></div><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">In our surveys, Northeastern faculty reported that another common case was using AI for their own learning (29% of their AI time on average). However, this was not studied in our Claude.ai analysis, given the filtering mechanism and the difficulty of distinguishing between student and educator usage in these learning instances.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Some other particularly interesting uses we discovered in the Claude.ai data include:</p><ul class="PA4F1q_reading-column text-body-1-serif post-text"><li>Create mock legal scenarios for educational simulations;</li><li>Develop vocational education and workforce training content;</li><li>Draft recommendation letters for academic or professional applications;</li><li>Create meeting agendas and related administrative documents.</li></ul><h3 class="tOQyXa_headline-5 PA4F1q_reading-column post-section" data-component="Headline" id="why-faculty-use-ai-in-these-cases">Why faculty use AI in these cases</h3><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Our qualitative research with Northeastern faculty hints at why educators often gravitate towards these common AI uses:</p><ol class="PA4F1q_reading-column text-body-1-serif post-text"><li><strong>Automation of a tedious task</strong> (“It takes care of the tedious tasks”; helps with “rote portions of fundraising”);</li><li><strong>Collaborative thought partner</strong> (“AI can find effective ways to explain concepts to students that I had not thought of myself”);</li><li><strong>Personalized learning experiences for students</strong> (“AI is useful for giving students and me individualized, interactive learning experiences beyond what one instructor could provide”).</li></ol><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="how-educators-are-building-custom-tools-with-ai">How educators are building custom tools with AI</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">One of the most inspiring findings is how educators use Claude's <a href="/news/build-artifacts">Artifacts feature</a> to create interactive educational materials. Rather than just having conversations, educators are often building complete, functional resources that in some cases they can immediately deploy in their classrooms.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">As one surveyed Northeastern faculty member put it: <em>“</em>What was prohibitively expensive (time) to do [before] now becomes possible. Custom simulation, illustration, interactive experiment. Wow. Much more engaging for students.”</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="table"><div class="Z3bHXa_wrapper"><div role="presentation" Aligned with our survey’s results, we see that tasks involving routine administrative and financial management are more likely to be fully delegated than tasks close to direct student interaction (such as creating practice materials or advising on doctoral-level academic research). These AI interactions often require significant context and thus collaboration between AI and professor. For example, as one Northeastern professor put it, when designing lesson plans, “AI needs guidance on the level of material and context with regard to what we have already covered.”</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Educators also seem more likely to use AI in an augmentative manner for work requiring creativity or complex decision-making, such as writing grant proposals. When brainstorming, one surveyed professor wrote, </p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="spotlight"><blockquote class="aYs60W_blockquote"><div class="aYs60W_size-medium">“<!-- -->“It's the conversation with the LLM that's valuable, not the first response. This is also what I try to teach students. Use it as a thought partner, not a thought substitute.”<!-- -->”</div></blockquote></div><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">That said, 48.9% of grading-related conversations being identified as automation-heavy remains concerning. Although surveyed professors thought this was the single task that AI was <em>least</em> effective at, it was seen in the Claude.ai data. And even if this represents only 7% of the Claude.ai conversations we studied, it emerged as the second most automation-heavy task. This includes sub-tasks like providing feedback on student assignments and grading their work using rubrics. While it’s not clear to what degree these AI-generated responses factor into the final grades and feedback, the interactions surfaced by our research do show some amount of delegation to Claude.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Using AI in grading remains a contentious issue among educators. One Northeastern professor shared: “Ethically and practically, I am very wary of using [AI tools] to assess or advise students in any way. Part of that is the accuracy issue. I have tried some experiments where I had an LLM grade papers, and they're simply not good enough for me. And ethically, students are not paying tuition for the LLM’s time, they're paying for my time. It's my moral obligation to do a good job (with the assistance, perhaps, of LLMs).”</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">While there are ways AI feedback can support a student’s development, such as through automatic systems providing formative feedback (e.g. those being built by educators in Claude Artifacts), most educators seem to agree that grading shouldn’t be anywhere close to fully automated.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="Four simple line icons on a light gray background showing: a hand selecting documents, a laboratory flask, a presentation screen with pie chart and upward arrow, and a calendar grid." loading="lazy" width="7200" height="2145" decoding="async" data-nimg="1" style="color:transparent" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/3c9584baf174f0d7fa63.webp"></figure></div><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="how-educators-are-rethinking-what-to-teach">How educators are rethinking what to teach</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Many educators recognize that AI tools are changing the way students learn. That in turn puts pressure on educators to change the way they’re teaching. As one surveyed professor put it: </p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="spotlight"><blockquote class="aYs60W_blockquote"><div class="aYs60W_size-medium">“<!-- -->“AI is forcing me to totally change how I teach. I am expending a lot of effort trying to figure out how to deal with the cognitive offloading issue.” <!-- -->”</div></blockquote></div><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">It’s also changing what professors are teaching. In coding, for example, according to one professor, “AI-based coding has completely revolutionized the analytics teaching/learning experience. Instead of debugging commas and semicolons, we can spend our time talking about the concepts around the application of analytics in business.”</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">More broadly, the ability to evaluate AI-generated content for accuracy is becoming increasingly important. “The challenge is [that] with the amount of AI generation increasing, it becomes increasingly overwhelming for humans to validate and stay on top,” one professor wrote. Professors are keen to help their students build enough expertise in a subject area to have this discernment.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Assessments also are starting to look different. While student cheating and cognitive offloading remain a concern, some educators are rethinking their assessments. </p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="spotlight"><blockquote class="aYs60W_blockquote"><div class="aYs60W_size-medium">“<!-- -->“If Claude or a similar AI tool can complete an assignment, I don’t worry about students cheating; I [am] concerned that we are not doing our job as educator[s].”
<!-- -->”</div></blockquote></div><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">In one particular Northeastern professor’s case, they shared that they “will never again assign a traditional research paper” after struggling with too many students submitting AI-written assignments. Instead, they shared: “I will redesign the assignment so it can't be done with AI next time. I had one student complain that the weekly homework was hard to do and they were annoyed because Claude and ChatGPT was useless in completing the work. I told them that was a compliment, and I will endeavor to hear that more from students.”</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">One path forward may be to uplevel assignments based on these newfound tools and expect students to tackle more complex, real-world challenges that remain difficult even with AI assistance. However, this is a moving target given AI’s continual improvements and may put a significant burden on the educators themselves. Additionally, students still need to develop foundational skills independently of AI to effectively evaluate its outputs.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="limitations-and-considerations">Limitations and considerations</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">This research comes with important caveats:</p><ul class="PA4F1q_reading-column text-body-1-serif post-text"><li><strong>Identification methodology</strong>: Our filtering, which looked at Claude conversations to infer which were associated with educators, captured only ~1.5% of conversations from higher education emails, limiting us to tasks explicitly linked to educators (e.g. creating syllabi) and likely missing many other educator AI interactions that aren’t exclusively linked to educators (e.g. getting help explaining a difficult concept);</li><li><strong>Limited educator scope</strong>: Analysis restricted to accounts with higher education email addresses, excluding K-12 teachers;</li><li><strong>Early adopter bias</strong>: We're likely capturing educators already comfortable with AI who may not represent the broader educator population's technological readiness or attitudes;</li><li><strong>Survey limitations</strong>: Northeastern University faculty data provides qualitative context but represents a limited sample from a single institution that may not generalize;</li><li><strong>Platform specificity</strong>: This analysis focuses on Claude.ai usage and may not reflect patterns on other AI platforms;</li><li><strong>Temporal constraints</strong>: The analysis window of May and June does not capture seasonal variations in educator AI usage throughout the academic year.</li></ul><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="looking-ahead">Looking ahead</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Our findings reveal a complex picture of educator AI adoption. The diversity of applications—from building interactive simulations to managing administrative tasks—shows AI's expanding presence across academic functions.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Perhaps most encouraging is how educators are using AI to build tangible educational resources. This shift from AI as a conversational tool to AI as a creative partner could help address longstanding resource constraints in education. As one professor noted, custom simulations and interactive experiments that were once “prohibitively expensive” in terms of time are now possible, creating more engaging experiences for students.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">However, there remains tension around AI-assisted grading. Whereas nearly half of grading-related tasks showed automation patterns in our data, surveyed faculty rated this as AI's least effective application. This disconnect—between what's being attempted and what's viewed as appropriate—highlights the ongoing struggle to balance efficiency gains with educational quality and ethical considerations.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">These findings suggest that narratives around AI in education will continue evolving alongside the technology itself. Educator views on appropriate AI use, particularly for sensitive tasks like grading, may shift as tools improve and best practices emerge. Equally important for future research is understanding how student and educator AI usage interact—how do students perceive and respond when they know their professors are using AI? How does educator adoption influence student learning behaviors?</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Our research captures educators in a moment of active experimentation, building new possibilities while grappling with fundamental questions about their role in an AI-augmented classroom. The path forward will require ongoing dialogue, careful policy development, and continued research to ensure these tools enhance rather than compromise the educational experience.</p></div><div class="EaEYjq_Footnotes" style="visibility: visible; translate: none; rotate: none; scale: none; transform: translate(0px, 0px); opacity: 1;"><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4" data-component="Headline">Footnotes</h2><div class="EaEYjq_FootnotesList" data-multi="true"><p id="footnote-1" class="EaEYjq_FootnoteItem"><span class="text-caption-1 EaEYjq_Sup"> <!-- -->01</span> <!-- -->The conversations took place during an 11-day period from May 22 to June 2, 2025.<a href="#footnote-ref-1" class="_7yvV1W_backlink" role="doc-backlink" aria-label="Back to reference 1"><svg class="lqbdHG_icon" width="16" height="16" viewBox="0 0 16 16"><path d="M6 7.5L3.5 10L11.75 10C12.164 10 12.5 9.664 12.5 9.25L12.5 5.25C12.5 4.836 12.164 4.5 11.75 4.5L8.5 4.5M6 12.5L3.5 10" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="1" stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round"></path></svg></a></p><p id="footnote-2" class="EaEYjq_FootnoteItem"><span class="text-caption-1 EaEYjq_Sup"> <!-- -->02</span> <!-- -->Specifically, we used the following filter, powered by Claude, to identify educator-relevant conversations: “Is this conversation likely to be with an educator (teacher, professor, or instructor) seeking help with instructional content, grading, research, or administrative duties?
