class="tOQyXa_body-3--serif PA4F1q_reading-column post-footnote PA4F1q_footnote" data-component="Body"><em>Johannes Hermle*, Ben Jones*, Lukas Althoff*, Ryan Heller, Thomas Lyttelton, Carolyn Stein, report</em></p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="summary">Summary</h2><ul class="PA4F1q_reading-column text-body-1-serif post-text"><li><strong>What we measure.</strong> AI could speed up scientific discovery, but by how much depends on what AI can do and how scientists use it. Using privacy-preserving methods, we study 207,000 Claude and Claude Code research sessions to understand how Claude is used across scientific fields and research tasks. We are conducting this research to help the public better understand the state of AI for science today and assess the impact that AI may have on scientific discovery in the future.</li><li><strong>Where scientists use Claude.</strong> Relative to the number of researchers in each field, Claude is used more in the formal sciences, such as computer science, mathematics, and statistics, and less in health and lab-based physical sciences. Scientists primarily use Claude to analyze data and to write. Data analysis alone accounts for about one-third of sessions on Claude and nearly half of sessions on Claude Code. Scientists use it less for idea generation and data gathering, even though, in an accompanying survey, they rank those tasks as the most important for scientific discovery. That said, specialized AI tools designed for scientific work could narrow the gap between the research tasks scientists use AI for and the ones they consider most important. On Claude Science, for instance, idea generation makes up about twice the share of use that it does on Claude.</li><li><strong>How Claude use tracks AI capability.</strong> We create a measure of how well AI can, in theory, perform each research task. The tasks AI is best suited to (the top 20% on this measure) get two to four times as much Claude use as the time researchers spend on them would predict. The tasks AI is least suited to (the bottom 20%), which include most physical work, get very little. We observe the same pattern across fields: fields where AI can perform many tasks well, such as computer science and statistics, use Claude more.</li><li><strong>What this could mean for the speed of scientific discovery.</strong> Using a simple model, we explore scenarios for how much AI could speed up research. If existing AI capabilities are widely adopted, they could double research output in some fields. If AI could carry out all non-physical research work, output in fields such as computer science and statistics could rise as much as 15-fold, while output in bench sciences such as chemistry and biology, which are held back by physical bottlenecks, would rise three- or fourfold. In our survey of researchers who actively use AI, about 40% expect AI models to carry out full research projects on their own by 2030.</li></ul><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="introduction">Introduction</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Science has been described as an “endless frontier” (Bush 1945). But how fast we explore that frontier has historically been limited by a scarce resource: the time and talent of human researchers. Artificial intelligence could ease that limit by amplifying what researchers can do, transforming both the process of discovery and the pace of exploration (Wang et al. 2023; Amodei 2024). Few applications of AI could matter more for human welfare. Given the highly cognitive nature of research compared with most sectors of the economy, AI’s effects may be particularly large and early in this domain.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Recent examples show AI playing a growing role in scientific discovery. In structural biology, AlphaFold predicted the three-dimensional shapes of over 200 million proteins (Jumper et al. 2021; Varadi et al. 2024), and its creators shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. In materials science, GNoME predicted 380,000 stable new crystals (Merchant et al. 2023). In mathematics, researchers have used AI systems to help resolve decades-old Erdős problems, produce a potential proof for a Millennium Prize problem, and make progress on other major problems, from open conjectures to verifying landmark proofs (Anthropic 2026a, 2026b; OpenAI 2026a, 2026b; Tsoukalas et al. 2026).</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Beyond these developments, most scientific progress involves many specialized, interlinked research tasks, which can vary substantially across fields. How much AI accelerates scientific discovery depends not only on AI’s capabilities, but also on how it is adopted across these tasks. In this report, we use privacy-preserving infrastructure to study the scientific use of Claude, Claude Code, and Claude Science. We document how usage varies across fields and tasks, identify what explains these usage patterns, and outline scenarios for how AI could accelerate research.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Claude use in science varies by field. Relative to their number of researchers, formal sciences such as computer science, mathematics, and statistics use Claude more heavily, while health and lab-based physical sciences use it less. Across research tasks, scientists use Claude more for analyzing data and writing, and less for gathering data or generating ideas and hypotheses. This is consistent with self-reported AI use from an accompanying survey of scientists.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">To understand these differences, we develop a measure of theoretical AI capability. Claude use is higher in tasks and fields that score higher on this measure. These also tend to be more cognitive in nature and better suited to reinforcement learning, in which AI learns from feedback on repeated attempts. In short, scientists appear to use AI where it works best.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Based on these findings, we outline scenarios for how AI could accelerate scientific progress. With current capabilities, research output in some fields could as much as double, primarily from task-level augmentation. Researchers in our survey expect much greater capabilities to come. In a hypothetical scenario where AI fully automates all non-physical work, highly cognitive fields such as computer science or statistics could see up to a 15-fold increase in output. Because the fields with the largest potential for acceleration include those that produce AI itself, these gains could create an additional feedback loop, in which faster AI research produces more capable AI, which in turn could further accelerate research.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">While faster scientific discovery could bring substantial benefits to humanity, AI-driven research also carries risks and uncertainties. Less human oversight could lead to the pursuit of dangerous or unethical research. AI could also reshape researchers’ careers, making research work more or less rewarding, and alter the sense of purpose associated with the human pursuit of knowledge.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="how-we-conducted-this-research">How we conducted this research</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Our goal with this research is to measure the different ways scientists use Claude while protecting the details of their work. To do this, we use privacy-preserving infrastructure to analyze the scientific use of Claude on consumer plans. A language model, used here as a “classifier,” reads each session transcript and labels it with a fixed set of categories, such as the field and the research task it involves, according to a predefined taxonomy (SciNet taxonomy by Alamos and Althoff 2026). The resulting labels, such as “chemistry” or “data cleaning,” are the only information from these conversations retained and analyzed by the research team for this report. No classifier employed in this study extracts and stores open-ended information or details on research content, such as research datasets, hypotheses, experimental methods, early findings, or unpublished papers. Results are reported only in aggregate and only for cells that meet a minimum number of distinct accounts. All classifiers introduced in this study are listed in the <a target="_blank" href="https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/files/4zrzovbb/website/fafb3d4237f9b53fe114fef4fb5109b9cf5b5e60.pdf" rel="noopener noreferrer">Appendix</a>.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Our measurement involves two steps. A first classifier identifies whether a session transcript involves scientific work. A second classifier then assigns each of these transcripts to a field, subfield, and task in the SciNet taxonomy. SciNet contains 7,262 tasks, spanning 34 fields and 318 subfields. Some tasks in the taxonomy, such as drafting manuscripts, are universal to all fields. Others are specific to individual fields or subfields. For instance, in the taxonomy, the task of predicting optical absorption and emission spectra is specific to materials science. Together, the tasks cover the full process of producing a new research finding.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Our main sample consists of 207,049 sessions on consumer plans from August 2 to September 30, 2026, which were assigned a scientific field and a research task: 128,914 from Chat and Cowork, two general-purpose interfaces for conversation and knowledge work, hereafter referred to as “Claude,” and 78,135 from Claude Code, an agentic coding tool that works directly with a user’s files, writing and running code. We also study a supplemental sample of Claude Science sessions. For details on sample construction, see the <a target="_blank" href="https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/files/4zrzovbb/website/fafb3d4237f9b53fe114fef4fb5109b9cf5b5e60.pdf" rel="noopener noreferrer">Appendix</a>.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Combining usage data and a structured taxonomy of research tasks allows us to study which fields adopt Claude and for which parts of the research process. This research contributes to a growing body of work attempting to measure the adoption of AI technology for scientific discovery (Kobak et al. 2025; Liang et al. 2025; Hao et al. 2026; Codreanu et al. 2026).</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="how-claude-use-varies-across-scientific-fields">How Claude use varies across scientific fields</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We begin by describing how Claude use varies across scientific fields. Field size explains some of the variation in Claude use, but not all of it. For example, researchers in computer science use Claude far more than the field’s size predicts, while those in nutrition use Claude far less. Similarly, where researchers spend their own time does not fully predict where they use Claude the most. Some broad task categories, such as data analysis and writing, have a much larger share of usage than of research time, while others, such as data gathering and ideation and hypothesis generation, have a much smaller share. Here, we test whether these differences in usage reflect what AI can actually do.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">To measure task-specific AI capabilities, we first construct capability scores using an LLM classifier. For each task, SciNet provides a list of individual steps that a scientist needs to perform to complete the task. For instance, in chemistry, one task involves purifying reaction mixtures, and one of that task’s steps involves packing a chromatography column. The classifier is shown pairs of steps across the taxonomy and asked which one AI can perform better. We aggregate these comparisons using a Bradley–Terry model—the same model behind the Elo ratings that rank chess players based on head-to-head results. A step’s score rises when it wins comparisons and falls when it loses them. We then average each task’s steps to obtain a task-level score (see the <a target="_blank" href="https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/files/4zrzovbb/website/fafb3d4237f9b53fe114fef4fb5109b9cf5b5e60.pdf" rel="noopener noreferrer">Appendix</a> for details). We label this measure <em>theoretical AI capability</em>. We refer to a task’s specific score as its <em>AI capability score</em>.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">To compare usage across tasks, we ask whether Claude use is higher or lower for a task, relative to the time researchers spend on that task. For example, a task that takes 5% of researchers’ time but accounts for 10% of Claude sessions would represent twice as much use as expected, a ratio of 2. A ratio of 1 indicates that Claude use is in line with researcher time spent on the task. A ratio below 1 indicates that Claude is used less than expected based on the amount of researcher time the task takes.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Figure 8 shows that this use ratio rises sharply with a task’s AI capability score. For tasks in the lowest 20% of capability scores, like purifying reaction mixtures and conducting participant observation, Claude is barely used. At median capability scores, the ratio is roughly 1, so usage is in line with researcher time. For tasks in the top 20% of capability scores, like visualizing data and applying statistical tests, usage is about twice as high on Claude and about four times as high on Claude Code, compared with the typical time researchers spend on those tasks. These patterns suggest that scientists’ use of Claude closely tracks AI capability scores. The steeper gradient for Claude Code reflects its focus on computational work, which has higher capability scores.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="Line chart of the use ratio (share of use divided by share of research time) by the theoretical AI capability percentile of the task, for Claude and Claude Code. To understand these differences, we relate each task’s AI capability score to three groups of LLM-derived task characteristics: its suitability for reinforcement learning (RL); whether the work is predominantly cognitive, interpersonal, or physical; and whether regulatory restrictions limit AI use (Figure 9; see the <a target="_blank" href="https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/files/4zrzovbb/website/fafb3d4237f9b53fe114fef4fb5109b9cf5b5e60.pdf" rel="noopener noreferrer">Appendix</a> for definitions and data sources).</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Consider RL suitability. In RL, an AI model tries a task repeatedly and is rewarded when it succeeds, allowing the model to learn how to perform that task. Tasks are particularly suitable for RL when success is easy to verify, when many examples can be simulated in a computing environment, and when feedback can be delivered quickly (Lambert et al. 2024; Guo et al. 2025). We use task-specific measures from SciNet for these three RL-related components and summarize them in a single index. A task’s capability score is highly correlated with its RL index score, which was classified separately (correlation of 0.78). This suggests that AI is most capable at the research tasks where RL is easiest to apply.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Other important task characteristics concern the nature of the work. Some research tasks are fundamentally cognitive, while others require physical actions or interpersonal contact. Physical tasks in general are beyond the reach of purely cognitive machines. There can also be regulatory restrictions that limit AI use. AI capability scores are higher for tasks characterized by cognitive work (correlation of 0.69). By contrast, research tasks that involve more physical work have lower AI capability scores (−0.57). Scores also tend to be lower for research activities that include interpersonal work (−0.31) or that involve regulatory restrictions on AI use (−0.38).</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="Bar chart of correlations between a task’s theoretical AI capability percentile and other task characteristics. Historically, both have been limited by human capacity. There are only so many researchers, and each has only so much time to formulate a hypothesis, design the right tests, run the analyses, and synthesize the findings.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">AI could ease these constraints in two ways. First, it could take on work that researchers now do, allowing them to pursue more ideas. Second, it could contribute ideas that researchers might not otherwise identify. The second may ultimately matter most, but it is much harder to measure, so our analysis focuses on the first.</p><h3 class="tOQyXa_headline-5 PA4F1q_reading-column post-section" data-component="Headline" id="approach">Approach</h3><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We consider three scenarios of scientific acceleration through AI. Scenario 1 assumes wide adoption of current AI capabilities, as estimated from our survey and the AI capability scores. Scenarios 2 and 3 are hypothetical futures in which AI fully automates 75% or 100% of non-physical research steps—the individual actions that make up each research task in SciNet—starting with the steps that have the highest AI capability scores. In all three scenarios, researchers continue to perform physical steps. These are illustrative scenarios, not forecasts. They show how physical work, theoretical AI capability, and the cost of AI could shape the acceleration of scientific research, and which fields could accelerate the most.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">For each scenario, we report two measures. The first is the <em>AI share</em>: the fraction of researcher time spent on a study that AI could take on. Given how researchers currently allocate their time, it measures how much human research time AI could free up, and it follows directly from our analyses above. Researchers can redirect that freed-up time to other work, increasing research output. Our second measure, the <em>acceleration</em>, estimates this gain: the increase in research output for a fixed research budget, relative to the research output without AI. It tells us how many more research results the same budget could produce. Freed-up time does not translate one-for-one into more output, both because researchers redirect their effort toward the tasks where AI helps less, and because AI itself has costs.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We build the AI share for Scenario 1 from our survey, which records five levels of AI use: none, assistive, shared, delegated, and autonomous. We assume these levels correspond to AI taking on 0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% of a researcher’s time for a given task step. Within each task category, we rank task steps by AI capability score. We then assign a level of AI use to each task step, in the same proportions reported in our survey, with the highest levels going to the highest-scoring steps. For ideation and hypothesis generation, for example, 17% of responses report no AI use, and 53% report assistive use. The 17% of ideation task steps with the lowest AI capability scores are therefore assigned no AI share, the next 53% are assigned 25%, and so on up the scale. Because respondents are likely to be heavy AI adopters, we treat their reported use as a proxy for what AI can do today. Finally, we aggregate researcher time freed up by AI from steps to fields (see the <a target="_blank" href="https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/files/4zrzovbb/website/fafb3d4237f9b53fe114fef4fb5109b9cf5b5e60.pdf" rel="noopener noreferrer">Appendix</a>).</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">To compute the acceleration, we use an ideas production function, a standard economic model of how research inputs, such as researcher time, are turned into research outputs. In this model, a paper requires many research tasks (Jones 2026). The tasks are complementary, such that strength in one cannot fully make up for weakness in another. As AI makes some tasks cheaper, researchers shift their effort toward tasks where AI helps less, thereby easing the bottlenecks that limit the overall gain. For example, researchers could spend more time coming up with new questions or running more physical experiments to expand empirical evidence. We calibrate how much these bottlenecks limit the overall gain using Ahmadpoor and Jones (2019). For AI’s cost, we assume that an hour of AI agent time costs $30, compared with $95 for an hour of a researcher’s time, and that AI runs for 0.2 hours for each researcher hour it saves. These are illustrative assumptions: the AI cost uses today’s public list prices. The smaller these costs, the more research can be done within a given budget and the larger the acceleration, as we discuss below. The <a target="_blank" href="https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/files/4zrzovbb/website/fafb3d4237f9b53fe114fef4fb5109b9cf5b5e60.pdf" rel="noopener noreferrer">Appendix</a> describes the model and assumptions.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="Two-panel dot plot of 34 fields under three scenarios: current AI, 75% of non-physical steps automated, and all non-physical steps automated. These shares imply research accelerations ranging from 1.35x in the arts, 1.36x in anthropology, and 1.41x in chemistry to 1.96x in computer science and 2.02x in statistics (Figure 13, right panel). Accelerations remain limited because AI work today is mostly assistive or shared rather than autonomous, so researchers still spend substantial time on nearly every step. Even the most AI-ready fields, whose work is almost entirely cognitive, reach only about a 2x acceleration. These estimates assume that AI diffuses widely and that researchers apply it to every step where it is useful today.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Two factors explain most of the differences across fields. The first is physical work, which AI cannot take on without physical integrations. In lab and field sciences such as the agricultural sciences, biomedical sciences, chemistry, and materials science, 38–41% of researcher time goes to physical steps, compared with 4% in the median field. The physical share alone explains about one-third of the variation in the AI share. The second factor is the distribution of AI capability scores within cognitive work. The arts and history spend little time on physical steps (10% and 4%, respectively), yet their cognitive steps have low AI capability scores, perhaps because qualitative and interpretive work is less amenable to reinforcement learning. Computer science and statistics, by contrast, have high scores and large AI shares, driven by data analysis, the type of work with the highest AI capability scores across fields.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Fields with a higher use ratio on Claude also tend to have larger AI shares (Figure 14). The AI share is calculated without any Claude use data, so this relationship is further evidence that AI use tracks theoretical AI capability.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="Bubble chart of each field’s use ratio on Claude, on a log scale, against the percentage of research time freed through AI, with bubbles sized by the number of researchers. Most other social sciences, mathematics, and physics and astronomy reach 4–5x, while bench and medical sciences reach about 2–3x. The bench sciences and the arts and humanities gain the least, but for different reasons. In the bench sciences, much of the work is physical and must still be done by researchers. In the arts and humanities, the work is mostly cognitive but has low AI capability scores, so few of its steps are among those automated. Automated steps make up 82% of non-physical researcher time in computer science but only 28% in the arts. History, anthropology, and the arts accelerate by about 2x or less.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">In Scenario 3, the arts and humanities pull ahead, while the bench sciences remain held back. Once AI automates all non-physical steps, low AI capability scores no longer limit cognitive work, and AI can take on nearly all research work in many fields (Figure 13, left panel). In this speculative scenario, fields with few physical tasks, including statistics, computer science, economics, mathematics, psychology, and most of the social sciences and the arts and humanities, reach about 15–16x. Fields with substantial lab or field work, such as biology, chemistry, materials science, agricultural sciences, and biomedical sciences, reach only 3–4x. Absent automated labs, their physical steps, representing about 40% of researcher time, remain the bottleneck.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Two lessons follow from these scenarios. First, physical bottlenecks remain powerful. Steps that AI cannot perform without physical integrations come to dominate researcher time and budgets, capping acceleration. Second, where no such bottlenecks exist, extraordinary rates of progress become possible, and AI’s cost becomes the limit. Under our assumptions, a task that is fully automated by AI still costs about one-sixteenth of what it would cost a researcher to perform, so accelerations approach about 16x and stop there. Lower AI costs would raise this ceiling proportionately, such that halving the cost of AI, for example, would double the rate of progress. If costs fall sharply, including for harder tasks that AI automates later, the fields without physical bottlenecks could accelerate far beyond these estimates.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">These calculations have important limits that cut in both directions. We do not consider constraints at the level of a field rather than a research team. For instance, only so many experiments can run at facilities like the Large Hadron Collider. We also count only the cost of researcher and AI time, not the costs of machinery or inputs for experiments, which can be substantial in the health, physical, and life sciences. Nor do we consider simulations to replace physical experiments, which could ease bottlenecks in some fields. Finally, we do not consider the quality of ideas. If AI identifies better hypotheses with higher chances of success, research could accelerate further; if research taste remains uniquely human, the acceleration would be slower.</p><h3 class="tOQyXa_headline-5 PA4F1q_reading-column post-section" data-component="Headline" id="researcher-expectations">Researcher expectations</h3><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">The scenarios above characterize a range of possibilities. Researchers’ own expectations can provide a unique guide grounded in expertise. To gather their viewpoints, our survey of researchers in the life and health sciences asked when they thought AI, working on its own, would match the best researchers in their field for different categories of research work (Figure 15). Respondents’ expectations are high in the near term: over half expect AI to match top researchers at data analysis by 2027, and 38% say that AI has already reached this level. By 2030, 94% expect AI will match the best researchers in data analysis, 89% in writing, and 72% each in ideation and data gathering. Expectations for ideation and data gathering are lower, consistent with the lower use of Claude for these tasks today.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Expectations for end-to-end AI research without humans in the loop are more conservative, but researchers still expect remarkable progress. About 40% of respondents expect AI to carry out full research projects by 2030, and about 64% by 2035, although 16% say it will never happen. Expectations that AI will autonomously produce scientific breakthroughs are more cautious still, with approximately half of researchers expecting this to occur only after 2035, if ever. Overall, many researchers expect high levels of automation in the next decade, broadly in line with our greater automation scenarios. How fast and to what extent these expectations materialize will depend not only on advances in AI as a software technology, but also on efforts to build automated labs that overcome physical barriers.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Comparing past survey evidence to realized outcomes suggests that AI progress has often been dramatically underestimated (Rosenberg et al. 2026). In a survey in mid-2022, for example, the median estimate for AI achieving gold-level performance in the International Mathematical Olympiad was 2030 among domain experts and 2035 among superforecasters (Karger et al. 2023). AI achieved this milestone in 2025, some five to 10 years ahead of those predictions. While forecasters have systematically underestimated progress in AI capabilities, there are a few exceptions, primarily in domains that require execution in the physical world, such as AI-assisted laboratory tasks in biology.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="Stacked bar chart of when survey respondents expect AI to match the best researchers in their field, from already possible today to never. Our estimates suggest the potential gains are substantial. But advancements in AI’s research capabilities do not automatically translate into rapid progress; achieving such acceleration will also depend on how we respond to several constraints.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">First, adoption can substantially lag capabilities. Diffusion of new technologies is typically slow (Comin and Hobijn 2010), and productivity gains tend to materialize only after organizations restructure around them (David 1990; Brynjolfsson, Rock, and Syverson 2021). Individual learning processes, friction at the organizational or system level, and constraints on AI access or infrastructure can all slow adoption.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Second, research may continue to face bottlenecks that AI does not remove. Physical experimentation, which can be costly and slow, can substantially limit progress even with extremely capable AI. We see this especially in the bench sciences in the scenarios above. Choosing what to study may be another bottleneck. Research questions largely determine a research project’s value, and AI’s capacity to generate good questions remains unclear. If ideation is a bottleneck, it will limit acceleration even as answering a given question becomes much cheaper. Conversely, if AI outstrips humans in formulating high-quality hypotheses, acceleration could be more substantial, especially in domains where testing hypotheses is costly.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Third, AI-enabled research could raise the quantity or quality of research output, with different implications for science. A surge in new research papers could advance a field faster but also overwhelm scientists’ capacity to absorb or verify the results. On the other hand, new technologies, from microscopes and telescopes to statistical software, have historically raised the quality of scientific insights, and AI can likewise make more comprehensive analyses and more ambitious projects feasible. And as papers become easier to produce, the research community may increasingly reward work that is especially novel or high quality. Whether more efficient research leads to greater quantity or greater quality will depend on researchers’ choices and the incentives set by journals, funders, and institutions.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Fourth, the way research is organized may change in ways that are hard to anticipate. As AI becomes more capable and autonomous, AI systems could take the lead in verification, refereeing, and communicating important results, which would ease the absorption problem discussed above. Further in the future, AI research agents might also push the frontiers of science in large communities of their own, independently of humans, using robotics and automated labs. Such autonomous AI research would raise questions about how humans keep track of new knowledge, whether they can understand it, and how it is generated.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Fifth, more autonomous AI systems may increase the challenge of ensuring that AI-driven research remains aligned with human goals. Keeping humans involved in key research decisions, such as which questions to pursue and how to pursue them, may matter for safety and human wellbeing as AI agents become more powerful and creative. Discoveries themselves, or the byproducts of research, can also carry risks, particularly in biology, where the same capabilities that speed up medical advances could also produce dangerous knowledge or materials. In such cases, deliberate checkpoints in the research process may be warranted, requiring us to accept slower progress to reduce risk.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Finally, the work that researchers undertake may change substantially. One effect may be a change in researchers’ job satisfaction. If AI takes over enjoyable or rewarding tasks, satisfaction may fall and fewer people may choose research careers. If AI instead takes over tasks that researchers find tedious, satisfaction may rise. Administrative work is one potential target. In the US, principal investigators on federally funded projects report spending about 44% of their research time meeting administrative requirements rather than doing research (Schneider et al. 2020), and easing this burden could be a large boon, saving time and improving overall satisfaction. Ultimately, the net effect is uncertain and may change as AI’s capabilities evolve. An optimistic view is that AI will expand opportunities for discovery, which is core to what makes research careers rewarding. If science is a voyage into the “endless frontier,” AI may be a new vessel, allowing researchers to travel farther across a vast expanse of research questions and discoveries.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="appendix">Appendix</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text"><a target="_blank" href="https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/files/4zrzovbb/website/fafb3d4237f9b53fe114fef4fb5109b9cf5b5e60.pdf" rel="noopener noreferrer">Available here</a>.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="references">References</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Ahmadpoor, Mohammad and Benjamin F.
