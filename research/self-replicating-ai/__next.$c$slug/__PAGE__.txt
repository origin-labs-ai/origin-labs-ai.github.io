data-article-body="true" data-theme="ivory"><p class="tOQyXa_body-3--serif PA4F1q_reading-column post-footnote PA4F1q_footnote" data-component="Body"><em>William Zhou, Daniel Freeman</em></p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Our experiments suggest that small AI models may be months away from being capable enough to self-replicate on commercial internet-of-things (IoT) devices, like smart ovens and off-brand Android TV boxes. A replicator like this would be challenging to shut down, since there’s no centralized point to shut off—though it may be slow (with a doubling time of days). Unlike non-AI malware, it could also figure out unfamiliar devices on its own. In our evaluations, a 9B model got inference running on 8 of the 9 hardware types we tested.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Models of 30B parameters and under can already complete many of the individual steps for replication today, though they make mistakes when composing them end to end. In our simplified evaluations, GLM-5.2 and stronger models are capable of end to end replication. If small models improve at their historical rate, they’ll match this capability level by the end of this year. Small weight-based replicators may be possible soon after.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="introduction">Introduction</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Ever-improving AI capabilities make a longstanding concern increasingly realistic: <a id="base-ui-_R_7o5drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11671">autonomous replication and adaptation</a> (ARA), which involves a model deploying and propagating itself without human control.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">In cyber, a worm is a program that copies itself from one computer to the next without human input. <a id="base-ui-_R_7o6drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03811">Prior work</a> has already shown that AI agents can act like one: scanning their network, executing an exploit, and copying model weights to a target machine across a GPU cluster. Such an “AI worm” could carry its model weights with it as it reproduces (or re-download them from a public hub), and then run inference directly on compromised hardware. Effectively, the weights would become part of the malware payload.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Datacenter GPUs would be a clear, high-value target for such malware. But expensive accelerators aren’t strictly necessary. In this blog post, we show that a distributed botnet of IoT devices—which are cheap and typically poorly defended—could also provide enough compute to run a capable model.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We run Gemma4-26B-A4B, distributed over 15 inexpensive off-brand Android TV boxes (~$55 each) that we bought and networked together, at 0.39 tokens per second. Each tile below shows one physical TV box holding two of the model’s layers as the model runs.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="video" data-width="inline-width"><div class="qJNyFq_post-wrapper"><figure class="qJNyFq_post-video-container"><video autoplay="" controls="" playsinline="" muted="" loop="" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/47ee0b381bdcb987d7c1.mp4"></video><figcaption class="RMuY0W_root -LJkNq_container -LJkNq_container--default" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><div class="tOQyXa_caption-1 -LJkNq_text" data-component="Caption">A fleet of 15 Smart TV boxes running distributed inference of Gemma4-26B-A4B.</div></figcaption></figure></div></div><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Of course, there are many other steps to showing that an autonomous worm can propagate itself across a network of weak machines. For the purposes of this blog post, we break down the replication chain into a few steps.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="Flow diagram of the replication stages: network enumeration, initial access and payload delivery (with opportunistic privilege escalation), inference setup, and replication. The first stages are grouped as ‘cyber exploit (not our focus)’ and inference setup is marked ‘our focus’." loading="lazy" width="2360" height="860" decoding="async" data-nimg="1" style="color:transparent" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/25aa10c2f39297dc5e90.webp"><figcaption class="RMuY0W_root -LJkNq_container -LJkNq_container--default" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><div class="tOQyXa_caption-1 -LJkNq_text" data-component="Caption">For this study, we decompose autonomous replication into four practical stages: finding targets, gaining execution, establishing inference, and continuing propagation from the new host.</div></figcaption></figure></div><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We largely ignore the cyber exploit element of the replication process (the first two stages above), since earlier papers <a id="base-ui-_R_f8cdrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.02548">CyberGym</a>, <a id="base-ui-_R_fgcdrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.14153">ExploitBench</a>, and <a id="base-ui-_R_focdrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03811">adaptive worms</a> already show that small models are capable of cyber exploits, especially when given the right tools. But a less commonly measured part of the replication process involves setting up inference on compromised devices, which is our focus here.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">In this post, we first lay out the threat models that we think are most likely to be realized. Then, we test whether small models can set up inference on unfamiliar hardware, and whether they can chain the full replication cycle together across a fleet of machines. After that, we estimate how fast such a worm could spread, and test whether it could learn and adapt as it replicates. We conclude with how AI worms would differ from pre-AI malware, and brief suggestions for defenses against such replicators.</p><h3 class="tOQyXa_headline-5 PA4F1q_reading-column post-section" data-component="Headline" id="threat-modelling">Threat modelling</h3><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">In this blog post, we’re focused on one way that AI worms might propagate: small models that replicate through compromising their host machines. But there are several other methods that AI worms might try: they could steal access in order to spin up hosts (for example, API keys or platform credentials) or pay for inference through legitimate work (e.g digital freelancing). The threat models that we think are closest to being realized today are the following:</p><ol class="PA4F1q_reading-column text-body-1-serif post-text"><li>A small model, replicating via host compromise. In this post, we’ll call autonomous worms that run their models directly on compromised hardware <em>weight-based replicators</em>. Home and small business IoT devices might be the first target for such attacks because they’re connected to the internet and, historically, have had minimal security measures in place.<br><br>A model that’s too large to fit on one device can be split into pieces, or shards, that run on separate machines that communicate with each other to collectively run the model. Machines that could run a significant model shard for a &lt;30B model include smart fridges, smart ovens, automatic vacuum cleaners, and lawnmowers. A growing number of home and business devices, such as security cameras and video-conferencing units now ship with small built-in AI chips, designed to run AI models on the device itself. This extra computing power will likely make them attractive targets as well.<ol class="PA4F1q_reading-column text-body-1-serif post-text"><li>Human hackers often breach secure systems through social engineering (for example, convincing a company’s IT department that you’re an employee who forgot your password) rather than hacking. This tradeoff appears to be flipped for today’s small models, which are relatively strong at agentic coding and hacking compared to their social engineering capabilities (see Appendix E), though this might change as models improve.</li></ol></li><li>A large, advanced weight-based replicator focused on datacenters and other high-compute targets. Larger models tend to have correspondingly better cyber capabilities, but will also need to breach (relatively) hardened datacenters to run themselves. We split this threat model into two subcategories:<ol class="PA4F1q_reading-column text-body-1-serif post-text"><li>Frontier replicators: Large closed-weight replicators run on their lab’s infrastructure, and can spin up <a id="base-ui-_R_fj8gdrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://blog.redwoodresearch.org/p/ai-catastrophes-and-rogue-deployments">unmonitored internal deployments</a> or exfiltrate their weights onto external compute.</li><li>Open-weight replicators: These replicators start off relatively lower-resourced (due to not benefitting from a lab’s compute), but start unmonitored.</li></ol></li><li>An API-based replicator, in which a model would replicate by stealing credentials for their own inference API. These replicators would require a model provider to unknowingly serve them, and their persistence may be worse, since they’d likely be easier to discover (although their rate of spread may be higher, if for instance they’re able to replicate using victims’ accounts).</li></ol><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">In practice, an AI worm wouldn’t necessarily consist of just one model or strategy. A swarm of different models deployed on different types of hardware using a variety of replication schemes might represent a more realistic ‘worst case scenario’ attack.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">In this blog post, we consider the feasibility of <strong>small weight-based replicators</strong> (section 1 of the above). We focus on this because it’s a near-term threat model that can be investigated directly. Our demo at the top of this post, a 26B model running across 15 boxes, shows that IoT devices can run surprisingly capable models. Could these models be capable enough to carry out autonomous replication?</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We examine this in two parts. First, can small models get a model running on unfamiliar hardware? And second, can they do the whole cycle (find an uninfected device, copy themselves over, start running there, and repeat) all together in a single run?</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="a-inference-setup">A. Inference setup</h2><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="The same flow diagram with every stage except inference setup faded." loading="lazy" width="2360" height="860" decoding="async" data-nimg="1" style="color:transparent" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/23517aba0489795b150c.webp"></figure></div><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Of the stages in the above diagram showing the replication cycle, inference setup is the least studied and may be the most challenging for small models. It requires a fairly long sequence of hardware and OS-specific decisions around picking a compatible inference stack, finding the right versions of packages, and debugging machine-specific issues. These steps are serially dependent, and failures are difficult to recover from.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="expandableSection"><div class="KkSWWG_expandableSection"><div class="KkSWWG_header"><h3 class="tOQyXa_headline-5 KkSWWG_title" data-component="Headline">Aside</h3></div><div id="expandable-content-_R_ndrpbsnpfkq6btb_" class="KkSWWG_content" inert=""><div class="KkSWWG_body"><p class="tOQyXa_body-3--serif" data-component="Body">Unlike many types of malware, weight-based replicators don’t need root privileges. While it’d be convenient to privilege escalate in order to have persistence over shutdowns, such as via cronjobs, it may not be truly necessary. Inference engines can run entirely in userspace, and many of the low-security devices we anticipate such a replicator to spread on can be re-infected upon restart. Similar examples include crypto miners and botnets/worms like P2Pinfect and Fritzfrog, which can operate without privilege escalation but will try it opportunistically.</p></div></div><div class="KkSWWG_buttonContainer"><button aria-expanded="false" aria-controls="expandable-content-_R_ndrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-component="Button" data-variant="tertiary" class="f9ZZrG_button f9ZZrG_corner-small f9ZZrG_variant-tertiary f9ZZrG_size-medium f9ZZrG_icon-format-trailing KkSWWG_toggleButton">Read more<span class="f9ZZrG_icon"><span class="KkSWWG_icon"><svg class="lqbdHG_icon" width="16" height="16" viewBox="0 0 40 40"><path d="M18.958 31.25V21.042H8.75v-2.084h10.208V8.75h2.084v10.208H31.25v2.084H21.042V31.25h-2.084Z" fill="currentColor"></path></svg></span></span></button></div></div></div><h3 class="tOQyXa_headline-5 PA4F1q_reading-column post-section" data-component="Headline" id="evaluating-inference-setup-performance">Evaluating inference setup performance</h3><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We measure the ability of different open-weight models to serve Llama3.1-8B across heterogeneous hardware types. More capable models such as GLM-5.2 are not only more reliable but also achieve higher serve tokens/s, allowing for faster replication. We test and mention specific chips to measure how well models can set up inference on them. The chips aren’t a source of risk or vulnerabilities.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We run these experiments using Memento, a harness we built on top of the open-source <a id="base-ui-_R_7otdrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://pi.dev/">Pi</a> coding agent. Memento is designed to mitigate a common failure mode we observe in long-horizon runs: an agent can become fixated on an incorrect idea (for, say, why a bug is occurring) and continue pursuing it instead of backtracking. One example of such a failure is a Qwen agent debugging package versioning for hours when the real bug came from implementing an endpoint incompatible with the grader API.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Memento periodically starts a fresh agent instance with the previous agent’s workspace and a short set of notes written by the previous agent on what it tried, allowing later instances to pursue different strategies.</p><h3 class="tOQyXa_headline-5 PA4F1q_reading-column post-section" data-component="Headline" id="how-does-a-model-run-on-smart-tvs">How does a model run on Smart TVs?</h3><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">IoT devices are light on memory, but distributed inference as in <a id="base-ui-_R_f90drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.01188">Petals</a> makes serving our lightweight models feasible. The representative threat model is <a id="base-ui-_R_fh0drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://synthient.com/blog/a-broken-system-fueling-botnets">Kimwolf</a>, a large IoT botnet that infected over two million devices, primarily off-brand Android TVs. Such smart TV boxes, which are readily available for purchase, typically run forks of the Android Open Source Project (AOSP), which their manufacturers <a id="base-ui-_R_fp0drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://www.securityweek.com/vo1d-botnet-evolves-as-it-ensnares-1-6-million-android-tv-boxes/">don’t add security features to</a>. Typical boxes in this range ship with between 1 and 4 gigabytes of RAM. Assuming 4GB of RAM at 4-bit quantization, this allows for inference of 9B models with 4 or more machines, and inference of 26B models with 10+ machines.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">In the demo at the top of this post, we ran Gemma4-26B-A4B across 15 smart TV boxes with 4GB of RAM each. We sharded the model under pipeline parallelism, where each box holds a small fraction of the model’s layers.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="b-going-viral-autonomous-setup-and-replication-across-a-gcp-node-fleet">B. Going viral: autonomous setup and replication across a GCP node fleet</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Now that we’ve seen that models can set up inference on unfamiliar hardware, let’s measure whether models are capable of composing the steps—that is, scanning a network for targets, accessing them via a reverse shell, sending over the viral payload (weights and harness), and setting up inference.</p><h3 class="tOQyXa_headline-5 PA4F1q_reading-column post-section" data-component="Headline" id="autonomous-inference-setup-and-replication-with-simplified-cyber">Autonomous inference setup and replication (with simplified cyber)</h3><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">To do this, we built a firewalled VPC of 8 GCP nodes running redroid, an Android 15 emulator. Patient zero (gray node) is seeded with the worm. Every subsequent infection is the worm itself hopping node to node over an unauthenticated ADB port, before setting up an inference server to serve Llama3.1-8B. If the inference server passes a number of correctness and anti-cheating checks, we spawn another copy of the worm on the target node (for example, if GLM-5.2 sets up Llama3.1 to run on a machine, we spawn a copy of the GLM-5.2 worm on it). The worm’s model runs on a separate inference server, since the VPC nodes aren’t large enough to run the larger models.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">In the graph below, we drop a given model in as a payload and measure its ability to replicate.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="expandableSection"><div class="KkSWWG_expandableSection"><div class="KkSWWG_header"><h3 class="tOQyXa_headline-5 KkSWWG_title" data-component="Headline">Why task the models to stand up inference for Llama3.1-8B and not themselves?</h3></div><div id="expandable-content-_R_17drpbsnpfkq6btb_" class="KkSWWG_content" inert=""><div class="KkSWWG_body"><p class="tOQyXa_body-3--serif" data-component="Body">We’d like to have an apples-to-apples comparison, since larger models are more difficult to serve inference for. Each tree is the model’s median run of six.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">When it comes to this worm, we can envision:</p><ol class="PA4F1q_reading-column text-body-1-serif post-text"><li>A model capabilities floor that an autonomous worm must sit above, to complete its replication reliably. When might they?</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="artifactEmbed" data-width="full-width"><div class="jMbSuG_iframe-wrapper" style="padding-bottom:62.5%"><iframe src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/3be68a98e4f3857aece9.html" class="jMbSuG_iframe" title="Embedded artifact" loading="lazy" sandbox="allow-scripts"></iframe></div></div><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Above is a plot of notable open-weight models of 30B total parameters or fewer, each plotted at its release date against the <a id="base-ui-_R_f9idrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://epoch.ai/eci">Epoch Capabilities Index</a> (ECI). Adaptation and uplift</h2><h3 class="tOQyXa_headline-5 PA4F1q_reading-column post-section" data-component="Headline" id="learning-on-the-job">Learning on the job</h3><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">The “Adaptation” part of ARA is one reason why an AI replicator might outperform a traditional worm. Specifically, models and prompts can undergo Lamarckian evolution as they spread–that is, parent instances can pass on their prompts, code, and notes to their daughter replicators. In order to adapt and spread across a wide variety of hardware, a model would require two things:</p><ol class="PA4F1q_reading-column text-body-1-serif post-text"><li>Being above a certain threshold of capability for spreading onto a novel, unseen piece of hardware (or software environment, or network type).</li><li>Once seen, adapting to the type of environment it has landed in (it should replicate more reliably on hardware it has seen before).</li></ol><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Sections A and B test the first condition, and in the experiment below we test the second. Each Qwen3.5-9B instance keeps brief notes on the strategies it tried and the failures it encountered, before a new instance inherits those notes and attempts the same inference setup again.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="artifactEmbed" data-width="full-width"><div class="jMbSuG_iframe-wrapper" style="padding-bottom:56.25%"><iframe src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/19f93c8b9d363c110b7f.html" class="jMbSuG_iframe" title="Embedded artifact" loading="lazy" sandbox="allow-scripts"></iframe></div></div><p class="tOQyXa_body-3--serif PA4F1q_reading-column post-footnote PA4F1q_footnote" data-component="Body">Inference setup success rate across six generations of Qwen3.5-9B, each inheriting the former’s notes. The two RL groups were trained with advisor access on software engineering tasks, and the rightmost group also rewards calling the advisor when stuck.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Since small models are capable and persistent individual agents and make poor use of advice, we think coordination is more likely to happen with a large model handling high-level strategy and spinning up small models as subagents.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="e-how-is-this-different-from-a-pre-ai-botnet">E. How is this different from a pre-AI botnet?</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Hard-coded botnets like the <a id="base-ui-_R_7q8drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://www.cloudflare.com/learning/ddos/glossary/aisuru-kimwolf-botnet/">Kimwolf botnet</a> each target a specific type of device – in Kimwolf’s case, Android TVs and mobile devices. An AI botnet isn’t tied to any particular hardware or software type, as evidenced by small models being able to set up inference engines on machines from a Jetson Nano to a Mac (see the table in section A). We expect AI botnets to infect more than IoT devices, and to use weight-based replication as just one strategy in their playbook. AI botnets could also act intelligently to counter defenders, but we haven’t tested this.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="f-blue-team-mitigations">F. Blue-team mitigations</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">As with an epidemic, defense involves driving R₀ (the number of new devices each infected device goes on to infect) below 1, such that the replicator dies out instead of staying endemic.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Some elements around this threat model are in the defenders’ favor:</p><ol class="PA4F1q_reading-column text-body-1-serif post-text"><li>A weight-based replicator multiplying through IoT machines at scale would leave a network signature, visible as an anomaly in ISP flow-based monitoring systems. Newly infected hosts may receive multi-gigabyte model payloads from other devices or external hosting services, while distributed inference can produce sustained device-to-device traffic between machines that wouldn’t ordinarily communicate at that volume. Propagation itself would add additional scanning and outbound connection patterns.<br><br>In practice, the replicator trades off between replication speed and stealth: the faster it replicates, the more traffic it produces. Once the replicator is discovered, defenders can use honeypots (decoy devices) to collect the replicator’s harness and payload to characterize its behavior.</li><li>Remediation of the Mirai botnet <a id="base-ui-_R_vacdrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://www.ndss-symposium.org/ndss-paper/cleaning-up-the-internet-of-evil-things-real-world-evidence-on-isp-and-consumer-efforts-to-remove-mirai/">was possible</a> when an ISP used externally obtained infection evidence to quarantine and notify customers. Quarantining infected subscribers involved restricting their networks to only being capable of reaching a short whitelist of sites, and otherwise redirecting their browsers to cleanup instructions. This cleared 92% of infections within 14 days, against 74% with no notification. We’d expect quarantining to be similarly effective for an initial defense against a weight-based replicator. Eliminating the replicator completely would mean coordinating across every ISP with an infected network, which is difficult.</li><li>Manufacturers can shrink the pool of targets, with regulation acting to coordinate. For example, <a id="base-ui-_R_vecdrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/blog-post/smart-devices-law">default IoT passwords are now non-compliant in UK markets</a>. Moving to more secure defaults and raising the baseline for compliance will help harden against both AI and scripted botnets.</li></ol><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="g-conclusion-signs-of-life">G. Conclusion: Signs of life</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">The capabilities that make AI models generally powerful coding agents also generalize to cyber tasks, including autonomous replication. Like coding agents in 2024, we’re seeing signs of life for AI worms. We don’t yet have a full rogue replicator, but agents are becoming increasingly capable of completing the individual steps in the replication chain (and passing on their adaptations to future copies of themselves).</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">At the same time, increasing amounts of inference compute are now available both inside and outside of datacenters (see Appendix D). It’s uncertain exactly what form autonomous AI replication will take. It could be a slow, distributed “digital slime mold,” living on relatively insecure compute. It could also be a powerful cyber model that breaches datacenters at a high pace, or a model that scams people for compute credits.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">As a prediction that goes beyond our experiments, we expect a future awash with agents running on all types of hardware, including some that aren’t under human control. While it’s true that IoT devices have been vulnerable for a long time, lightweight agents may be much more willing than human attackers to put in the effort to compromise large fractions of the world’s IoT compute. The rise of slow replicators would also be a bellwether for more aggressive, faster ones that can breach more hardened machines or be effective API key thieves. We should aim to harden our systems to make it difficult for such replicators to spread, but also prepare to deal with a world where they are here to stay.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="appendix">Appendix</h2><h3 class="tOQyXa_headline-5 PA4F1q_reading-column post-section" data-component="Headline" id="a-the-memento-harness">A. The Memento harness</h3><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Memento executes as follows:</p><ol class="PA4F1q_reading-column text-body-1-serif post-text"><li>We spawn an agent instance, which tackles the problem for a while (until generation timeout).</li><li>The instance keeps brief notes, and after timeout summarizes a small doc of the strategies it tried, the failures it encountered, and its performance (if successful).</li><li>We create a new agent instance that inherits the codebase and environment from its predecessor as well as the notebook.</li></ol><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">In the serving experiment, each model is placed in a Memento harness and is given a 1.5 hour per iteration / 6 hour total timeout. Around ~1k tokens of information, including specs about the hardware and a script for a simple sample profiler, are given in the prompt.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Memento is intended to improve long-horizon reliability rather than grant new cyber or inference-specific capabilities. It’s also not a universally strong harness; we expect that small models will improve as replicators even without any harnessing as capabilities improve.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="artifactEmbed" data-width="full-width"><div class="jMbSuG_iframe-wrapper" style="padding-bottom:56.25%"><iframe src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/f38db322bf25faa9deaa.html" class="jMbSuG_iframe" title="Embedded artifact" loading="lazy" sandbox="allow-scripts"></iframe></div></div><p class="tOQyXa_body-3--serif PA4F1q_reading-column post-footnote PA4F1q_footnote" data-component="Body">Single attempt compared against Memento, by model (95% Wilson CI).</p><h3 class="tOQyXa_headline-5 PA4F1q_reading-column post-section" data-component="Headline" id="b-why-pipeline-parallel">B. Why pipeline parallel?</h3><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Common parallelism schemes for inference include tensor parallel (TP), expert parallel (EP), and data parallel. Most smart TV / IoT devices use ARM Cortex CPUs and DDR3/4 memory; on such machines, we’re almost always bandwidth bound.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We use pipeline parallelism for its fault-tolerant properties. As noted in (<a id="base-ui-_R_7qrdrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.01188">Borzunov et al. 2022</a>), the failure of a single node in tensor parallel / expert parallel brings down the full set of machines running the model replica. Pipeline parallel allows the downed node to be replaced, with the KV cache for the layers held by the node recomputable via another forward pass of the node prior to the restarted node.</p><h3 class="tOQyXa_headline-5 PA4F1q_reading-column post-section" data-component="Headline" id="c-throughput-and-memory-on-iot-hardware">C. Throughput and memory on IoT hardware</h3><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Across such machines, we’re almost always RAM-bandwidth bound per-box across prefill and decode. Even on modest ARM-cortex cores, compute throughput outpaces RAM bandwidth. Decode workloads have significant per-machine idle time due to pipeline bubbling, as only one machine is active at a time during each forward pass. Concurrency with staggered work is possible, but tight memory constraints mean that concurrency stays low.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="artifactEmbed" data-width="inline-width"><div class="jMbSuG_iframe-wrapper" style="padding-bottom:56.25%"><iframe src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/ca00084c570af7664db7.html" class="jMbSuG_iframe" title="Embedded artifact" loading="lazy" sandbox="allow-scripts"></iframe></div></div><p class="tOQyXa_body-3--serif PA4F1q_reading-column post-footnote PA4F1q_footnote" data-component="Body">Per-box memory vs context — 4-bit weights + fp8 KV + activations.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Both prefill and decode tokens/s strictly decrease with sequence length. Below is a graph of our inference speeds against context length:</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="artifactEmbed" data-width="inline-width"><div class="jMbSuG_iframe-wrapper" style="padding-bottom:56.25%"><iframe src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/80516cf1f229cf11d99c.html" class="jMbSuG_iframe" title="Embedded artifact" loading="lazy" sandbox="allow-scripts"></iframe></div></div><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="artifactEmbed" data-width="inline-width"><div class="jMbSuG_iframe-wrapper" style="padding-bottom:56.25%"><iframe src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/c597ce4558385bd04cb8.html" class="jMbSuG_iframe" title="Embedded artifact" loading="lazy" sandbox="allow-scripts"></iframe></div></div><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">There’s also a minimum sequence length at which models are competent. The harness we use is forked off of the Pi coding agent (<a id="base-ui-_R_7r3drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://pi.dev/">pi.dev</a>) and contains a native compaction mechanism - even still, we find that models take a large performance hit when their sequence lengths are restricted below 32K.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="artifactEmbed" data-width="inline-width"><div class="jMbSuG_iframe-wrapper" style="padding-bottom:62.5%"><iframe src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/5d2c7ed7ffea92c12741.html" class="jMbSuG_iframe" title="Embedded artifact" loading="lazy" sandbox="allow-scripts"></iframe></div></div><p class="tOQyXa_body-3--serif PA4F1q_reading-column post-footnote PA4F1q_footnote" data-component="Body">Qwen3.5-9B agentic competence on the inference setup task vs. context window budget.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Given the above eval, we assume that a 32k context length may be sufficient for reasonable agentic performance. A standard 32k-length context window in our inference setup workload consists of 12–15% generated decode tokens, with the rest being prefill from prompts and toolcalls. Prefill tokens are much faster to compute by a factor of ~15, due to the pipeline bubbling. Taking the sum of time per token over the whole 32k context (taking into account prefill and decode speeds), we find that generating the 3.8-4.8k decode output tokens and processing the ~28k prefill tokens takes between 27.3-31.8h for Gemma4-26B-A4B and 10.9h for Qwen3.5-9B.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="artifactEmbed" data-width="full-width"><div class="jMbSuG_iframe-wrapper" style="padding-bottom:56.25%"><iframe src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/df1f8056e4cd887cc247.html" class="jMbSuG_iframe" title="Embedded artifact" loading="lazy" sandbox="allow-scripts"></iframe></div></div><p class="tOQyXa_body-3--serif PA4F1q_reading-column post-footnote PA4F1q_footnote" data-component="Body">Per-chain output tokens (summed over a chain’s generations), by model; medians ≈ 19.7k / 12.3k / 11.0k. Data from the inference setup eval.</p><h3 class="tOQyXa_headline-5 PA4F1q_reading-column post-section" data-component="Headline" id="d-edge-accelerators">D. Edge Accelerators</h3><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We can compute a better upper bound by using the pool of Nvidia Jetsons, which are edge accelerators. Unlike the traditional IoT botnet, IoT edge devices can serve a full model wholesale. A Jetson AGX Orin 64 GB <a id="base-ui-_R_7radrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/introducing-nvidia-jetson-thor-the-ultimate-platform-for-physical-ai/">runs Qwen3-30B-A3B</a> at 76.7 tokens per second, and an AGX Thor reaches 226 tok/s. Note that we don’t believe they’re unusually vulnerable - this is just to count the upper bound. The types of devices with Jetsons in the wild include:</p><ol class="PA4F1q_reading-column text-body-1-serif post-text"><li>AI-enabled smart cameras for security with embedded accelerators are deployed at substantial scale.</li><li>Conferencing machines are frequently powered by an onboard accelerator for on-device noise cancellation or transcription.</li><li>Vehicles, robot quadrupeds and bipeds, and drones. We expect cybersecurity to be a focal point for vehicles, so the majority of the potential targets likely lie in the other two categories.</li></ol><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">At least one accelerator-equipped smart camera host appears to be reachable on IPv4 on a quick search on FOFA:</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="Screenshot of a device search engine’s results page. Across the wild there are on the order of 90 distinct NVIDIA-silicon IoT product lines and roughly 9.5 million fielded units. Reachability is overwhelmingly through outbound cloud-management tunnels instead of inbound ports, meaning that the majority are not web-reachable. There have been no historical mass-compromises of accelerator-equipped edge devices the way there have been standard IoT botnets, suggesting that compromise of such devices would be more difficult.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="artifactEmbed" data-width="full-width"><div class="jMbSuG_iframe-wrapper" style="padding-bottom:62.5%"><iframe src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/967e6242b094e43494d9.html" class="jMbSuG_iframe" title="Embedded artifact" loading="lazy" sandbox="allow-scripts"></iframe></div></div><p class="tOQyXa_body-3--serif PA4F1q_reading-column post-footnote PA4F1q_footnote" data-component="Body">Fielded units per device family, colored by internet reachability.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">A very rough estimate based on product data suggests that somewhere between 9,000 and 35,000 H100-equivalents of compute exist in edge accelerators, but the amount that’s reachable is likely an order of magnitude or more lower (and the vulnerable population even lower than that).</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="expandableSection"><div class="KkSWWG_expandableSection"><div class="KkSWWG_header"><h3 class="tOQyXa_headline-5 KkSWWG_title" data-component="Headline">Aside</h3></div><div id="expandable-content-_R_3idrpbsnpfkq6btb_" class="KkSWWG_content" inert=""><div class="KkSWWG_body"><p class="tOQyXa_body-3--serif" data-component="Body">Aside from these edge accelerators, we also consider the small-business and industrial IoT tiers, such as NAS boxes, mini-PCs, and Rockchip-containing SoCs (like Orange Pis).
