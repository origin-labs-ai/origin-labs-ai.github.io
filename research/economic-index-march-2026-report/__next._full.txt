class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">The Anthropic Economic Index uses our privacy-preserving data analysis <a href="/research/clio">system</a> to track how Claude is being used across the economy. It’s part of our effort to understand the economic impacts of AI as early as possible, so that researchers and policymakers have adequate time to prepare.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">This latest report studies Claude usage in February 2026, building on the economic primitives framework introduced in our <a href="/research/anthropic-economic-index-january-2026-report">previous report</a> (which used data from November 2025). Our sample covers February 5 to February 12, three months following the release of Claude Opus 4.5 and coincident with the release of Claude Opus 4.6.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We first document how usage has changed relative to our previous reports: the rate of augmentation, collaborative interaction where the AI complements the user’s abilities, increased slightly in both Claude.ai and API traffic. In Claude.ai, usage diversified, with the top 10 tasks accounting for a smaller share of usage last month than in November 2025. As a result of this diversification, the average conversation in Claude.ai had a slightly lower-wage task <a target="_blank" href="https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/files/4zrzovbb/website/f065d6e6f92c65df8244042c83d48872ea308c3a.pdf" rel="noopener noreferrer">than</a> in previous reports.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We then focus on an important determinant of Claude’s impact on the labor market and the broader economy: learning curves in Claude adoption. We present evidence that high-tenure users have developed habits and strategies that allow them to better harness Claude’s capabilities. Indeed, we document that more experienced users not only attempt higher-value tasks, but are also more likely to elicit successful responses in their conversations.</p><h4 class="tOQyXa_headline-6 PA4F1q_reading-column post-subsection" data-component="Headline" id="what-has-changed-since-our-last-report">What has changed since our last report</h4><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">In the first chapter, we revisit findings from our previous <a href="https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/096d94c1a91c6480806d8f24b2344c7e2a4bc666.pdf">Economic Index report</a>, published in January 2026. We find that:</p><ul class="PA4F1q_reading-column text-body-1-serif post-text"><li><strong>Use cases on Claude.ai diversified. </strong>Coding tasks continue to migrate from augmentative usage in Claude.ai to more automated workflows in our first-party API traffic.<sup class="caption PA4F1q_sup PA4F1q_footnote-marker"><a href="#footnote-1" id="footnote-ref-1" class="PA4F1q_footnote-ref" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup> In this report, Claude.ai usage was less concentrated: the top 10 tasks made up 19% of all traffic in February, down from 24% in November. That said, almost all tasks in this sample appeared in at least one of our previous samples. About 49% of jobs have seen at least a quarter of their tasks performed using Claude.</li><li><strong>Claude adoption broadened to lower-wage tasks. </strong>As use cases have diversified, the average economic value of work done on Claude—as measured by US wages paid to workers in the associated occupations—has decreased slightly. This is caused, mechanically, by a rise in personal queries around sports, product comparisons, and home maintenance. The pattern is consistent with a standard “adoption curve” story, in which early-adopters favor specific high-value uses like coding, and later adopters take on a much wider range of tasks.</li><li><strong>Inequality in global usage has persisted. </strong>Usage remains heavily concentrated: the top 20 countries account for 48% of all per-capita usage, up from 45%, underscoring a persistent gap in global adoption. However, Claude usage per capita continued to converge within the United States: the share of usage accounted for by the 10 highest usage states decreased from 40% to 38% since our last report.</li></ul><h4 class="tOQyXa_headline-6 PA4F1q_reading-column post-subsection" data-component="Headline" id="learning-curves">Learning curves</h4><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">A central finding in the Economic Index is that early adoption of Claude is very uneven: Claude is used more intensely in high-income countries, within the US in places with more knowledge workers, and for a relatively small set of specialized tasks and occupations.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">An important question is how inequality of adoption might determine where and to whom the benefits of AI will accrue. If, for example, effective AI use requires complementary skills and expertise—which we argued in our <a href="https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/096d94c1a91c6480806d8f24b2344c7e2a4bc666.pdf">previous report</a>—and if such skills can be acquired through use and experimentation, then the benefits from early adoption may be self-reinforcing.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">In our second chapter we investigate how users appear to shape the value that they get out of Claude: how they match model capability to the task at hand, and how usage patterns and outcomes shift with experience on the platform.</p><ul class="PA4F1q_reading-column text-body-1-serif post-text"><li><strong>Model selection matches the task. </strong>We show that users choose our most intelligent model class, Opus, for tasks that normally receive higher wages in the labor market. For example, among paying Claude.ai users, Opus is used 4 percentage points more than average for coding tasks and 7 percentage points less than average for tutoring-related tasks. This model switching is about twice as stark for API users. </li><li><strong>Higher tenure, higher success. </strong>In general, the most seasoned Claude users employ it more often for higher-education tasks and less often for personal use cases. For example, people who have been using Claude for 6 months or more have 10% fewer personal conversations and a 6% higher education level reflected in their inputs. Most strikingly, people in this higher-tenure group have a 10% higher success rate in their conversations, an association that is not explained by their task selection, country of origin, or other factors. While this could reflect sophistication of early adopters, it could also be evidence of learning-by-doing, where people get better at using Claude through experience.</li></ul><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="what-has-changed-since-our-last-report-2">What has changed since our last report</h2><h3 class="tOQyXa_headline-5 PA4F1q_reading-column post-section" data-component="Headline" id="diversification-of-use-cases-in-claudeai">Diversification of use cases in Claude.ai</h3><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We first look at the kinds of tasks that Claude is asked to perform. We use our <a id="base-ui-_R_f8fdrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13678" rel="noopener noreferrer">privacy-preserving system</a>, which allows us to describe behavior at an aggregated level without revealing the content of individual transcripts. Little is known about how AI users choose between different models, navigating tradeoffs around speed, performance, and cost. If users are calibrating to the task at hand, we should see Opus concentrated on harder, higher-value work.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Next, we study how usage differs according to tenure, finding differences across users who signed up at different times. This sheds light on learning curves: do experienced users get better over time? How does their usage differ? We find evidence consistent with learning-by-doing. Not only do higher-tenure users have greater success in their conversations, they also collaborate with Claude more, bring more challenging tasks to Claude, and are more likely to use Claude for work purposes and for a wider range of tasks.</p><h3 class="tOQyXa_headline-5 PA4F1q_reading-column post-section" data-component="Headline" id="model-selection">Model selection</h3><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">The different Claude model classes (Haiku, Sonnet, and Opus) offer tradeoffs in terms of cost, speed, and performance. The Opus class of models uses the most tokens and excels at complex tasks, but at a higher per-token price on our API. If users are aware of this and mindful of costs and usage limits, they should bring their most complicated and valuable tasks to Opus, while selecting other models for simpler tasks. This is broadly what we observe in the data.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Figure 2.1 below shows that, for paid Claude.ai accounts, which have access to all model classes, 55% of Computer and Mathematical tasks (like coding software) use Opus, compared to 45% of Educational tasks. Technical users may notice performance gains and actively switch away from Sonnet, the default. Or efficiency-minded users may learn to use Sonnet for simpler tasks to avoid hitting usage limits. Relatedly, the differences here could reflect that most educational tasks are already fairly easy for Sonnet, or that students are more likely to be mindful of usage limits.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="full-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="" loading="lazy" width="4417" height="2497" decoding="async" data-nimg="1" style="color:transparent" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/e94dc2b1b63de8401267.webp"><figcaption style="--figure-label:&quot;Figure 2.1&quot;" class="RMuY0W_root -LJkNq_container -LJkNq_container--default" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><div class="tOQyXa_caption-1 -LJkNq_text" data-component="Caption"><strong>Model choice and occupational domains. </strong>This plot shows how much more or less paid Claude.ai users select the Opus class of models depending on which occupational domain the task is in. Since then, the userbase on both Claude.ai and the API has grown rapidly. Our latest sample contains a mix of users, some of whom signed up for the very first Claude release, and some of whom signed up just the day before we measured their usage. In poorer countries with much lower adoption, these early adopters still dominate the user base.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">More casual usage emerges when AI has diffused to a broader share of the population. Indeed, among request clusters, tasks with highest mean tenure included: AI research, git operations, revising manuscripts, and startup fundraising. The tasks with the lowest average tenure have more simple workflows like writing haikus, checking sports scores, and suggesting food for a party.<sup class="caption PA4F1q_sup PA4F1q_footnote-marker"><a href="#footnote-11" id="footnote-ref-11" class="PA4F1q_footnote-ref" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Footnote 11">11</a></sup></p><h4 class="tOQyXa_headline-6 PA4F1q_reading-column post-subsection" data-component="Headline" id="experience-effects">Experience effects</h4><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We explore these relationships more in Figure 2.4 below, using the log-level data to control granularly for features of the conversation. In the top panel, specification (1) shows a simple bivariate regression with task success as the outcome and the long-tenure indicator as the predictor. Success is Claude’s assessment of whether the conversation was successful, <a href="https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/096d94c1a91c6480806d8f24b2344c7e2a4bc666.pdf">described in our previous report</a>. The plot shows that long-tenure users are about 5 percentage points more likely to have a successful conversation.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">This could reflect that higher-tenure users are better at prompting. But what if it reflects that they bring different tasks to Claude—ones more likely to be successful?</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">In Specification (2), we include fixed effects for specific O*NET tasks and request clusters. This amounts to comparing high- and low-tenure users within the same narrowly defined task, rather than across tasks. For instance, we have a request cluster called “Perform corporate financial analysis, valuation, and modeling for specific companies.” The fixed effects compare high- and low-tenure users within that cluster, and likewise within every other cluster. We would only observe a positive coefficient if, on average, long-tenure users are more successful in these within-task comparisons. This control moderates the effect somewhat, bringing it closer to 3 percentage points.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="" loading="lazy" width="4417" height="2497" decoding="async" data-nimg="1" style="color:transparent" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/72bc4165083b899d02e1.webp"><figcaption style="--figure-label:&quot;Figure 2.4&quot;" class="RMuY0W_root -LJkNq_container -LJkNq_container--default" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><div class="tOQyXa_caption-1 -LJkNq_text" data-component="Caption"><strong>The association between experience and success. </strong>This plot shows the results from regressing our binary success measure on an indicator for high tenure, with increasingly stringent controls. The coefficient is given in percentage points, and the whiskers give 95 percent confidence intervals. “Task FEs” indicates fixed effects for O*NET task and request cluster. Full controls adds model, use case, and country fixed effects.<br></div></figcaption></figure></div><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text"><br>Finally, we ask whether this relationship is affected by higher-tenure users selecting different models, communicating in different languages, having different use cases, or signing on in different countries. This regression yields a slightly higher impact of high tenure, suggesting a 4-percentage-point higher success rate accounting for the full controls.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">These results suggest that high-tenure users have more success in their Claude conversations, and that this is not due to simple factors like language or the task being performed. One intriguing potential explanation is that these users have better learned to extract what they want from AI. Facility with these platforms may be a key determinant of success that appears to scale with experience.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="discussion">Discussion</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">This report revisited the core measures we use to track Claude usage, and analyzed model selection and success for the first time. Since August 2025, 1P API usage has become more concentrated, with the top 10 O*NET tasks now accounting for 33% of traffic, up from 28%. Claude.ai tasks, on the other hand, have diversified since our November 2025 data. Faster adoption among low-usage states continued in the US, though at a slower pace than in the previous report. Low-adoption countries fell slightly further behind.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">With this report, we can begin to trace out how various economic primitives have changed. Coursework fell as a share of usage while personal conversations increased. We also note a slight decrease in the aggregate complexity of prompts in Claude.ai, with conversations in Claude.ai exhibiting less sophisticated inputs and shorter estimated completion times.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Overall, Claude is used for high-value, complex work that is not broadly representative of the US economy. But as the user base has grown, less remunerated tasks have become a slightly larger share of traffic. The average value of tasks, measured as the estimated wage paid to workers in occupations associated with those tasks, has declined on Claude.ai since our first report, while rising among API users. On both surfaces, users bring their most complex tasks to our more powerful model class, Opus. This inflection is stronger for API customers.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">More experienced users tend to use Claude more collaboratively, for more work-related reasons, in more complex tasks, and with more success. This pushes back against a hypothesis we made last year that automated use may be more typical of more experienced, sophisticated users; instead, we find that the most advanced users are more likely to iterate with Claude. It’s also consistent with learning-by-doing: the more time one spends using AI, the more effective one becomes at harnessing it.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">An alternative interpretation, of course, is that these results are driven by cohort effects or survivorship bias. Early adopters could be more technical. Those who continue using Claude could be those with tasks that it is distinctly well-equipped to do. But carefully controlled regressions rule out simple versions of this confounding, like that long-tenured users bring different kinds of tasks. Over time, we will be able to more cleanly isolate cohort and survivorship bias from learning-by-doing.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">These observed differences in success rates could deepen inequalities in the labor market. Economists have long noted the potential for <a id="base-ui-_R_fa7drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/0022051026976">skill-biased technological change</a>: innovations that raise wages for high-skill workers while depressing them for others. Our analysis in this report identifies a channel through which such skill-biased transformation may already be unfolding: early adopters with high-skill tasks have more successful interactions with Claude than later, less technical adopters. These early-adopting users may simultaneously be the <a href="/research/labor-market-impacts">most exposed</a> to AI-driven disruption and most aided by AI in these initial, augmentative waves of adoption.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="appendix">Appendix</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Available <a
