data-article-body="true" data-theme="ivory"><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Last October, the Anthropic Interpretability team published <a id="base-ui-_R_3q1drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://transformer-circuits.pub/2023/monosemantic-features" rel="noopener noreferrer">Towards Monosemanticity</a>, a paper applying the technique of dictionary learning to a small transformer model. In May this year, we published <a id="base-ui-_R_3s1drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://transformer-circuits.pub/2024/scaling-monosemanticity/" rel="noopener noreferrer">Scaling Monosemanticity</a>, where we applied the same technique to a model several orders of magnitude larger. We found tens of millions of “features”—combinations of neurons that relate to semantic concepts—in Claude 3 Sonnet, representing an important step forward in understanding the inner workings of AI models.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">To continue making this progress, we need more engineers.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">This might seem surprising if you've only read our early papers (for example <a id="base-ui-_R_3q3drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://transformer-circuits.pub/2021/framework/index.html" rel="noopener noreferrer">Frameworks</a> and <a id="base-ui-_R_3s3drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/toy_model/index.html" rel="noopener noreferrer">Toy Models of Superposition</a>), which required relatively little engineering. But reading the newer research should make clear the scale of the engineering challenge we face.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Below, we share two examples of the technical engineering questions that were involved in our latest research. These illustrate the kinds of problems our engineers are tackling right now, and help explain why we think engineering will be one of the major bottlenecks to progress in AI interpretability—and ultimately, AI safety—research.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">If you're an engineer, this post is aimed at you. If you’re inspired by the examples of engineering problems discussed below, we strongly encourage you to apply for our <a id="base-ui-_R_1u5drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://boards.greenhouse.io/anthropic/jobs/4020305008" rel="noopener noreferrer">Research Engineer role</a>.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="engineering-problem-1-distributed-shuffle">Engineering Problem 1: Distributed Shuffle</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Our Sparse Autoencoders—the tools we use to investigate “features”—are trained on the activations of transformers, and those activations need to be shuffled to stop them from learning spurious, order-dependent patterns. When we first started training sparse autoencoders, we could fit our training data on a single GPU and trivially shuffle it. But eventually, we wanted to scale beyond what could fit in memory (imagine starting with the easy task of shuffling a deck of cards, but then scaling it up to shuffling entire warehouses full of cards — it’s a much more difficult problem).</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">At this point, we could have implemented a distributed shuffle that scaled to petabytes. Instead, we decided on an approach we could implement quickly, but which didn't scale as well. We split our shuffle into <em>K</em> jobs where each job was responsible for 1/<em>K</em> of the shuffled output data. We generated a permutation, had each job do a streaming read of all of the training data, and then had it write out its share of the output. This allowed us to scale further, but the downside was obvious: each job had to read all of the training data. This first took hours, and later took days. By the time we were working on <a id="base-ui-_R_3u8drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://transformer-circuits.pub/2023/monosemantic-features/index.html" rel="noopener noreferrer">Towards Monosemanticity</a>, we had 100TB of training data (100 billion data points, each being 1KB) and shuffling had become a major headache.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Performing a distributed shuffle that scales isn’t a novel or cutting-edge problem. But it was just one of many engineering problems we had to solve quickly to make scientific progress.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">In this case, we found a <a id="base-ui-_R_7iadrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://blog.janestreet.com/how-to-shuffle-a-big-dataset/" rel="noopener noreferrer">helpful blog post</a> and extended the approach to many passes. For one pass, we have<em> N</em> jobs. Each job reads 1/<em>N</em> of the dataset, shuffles it, and writes out the data in K files each with 1/<em>NK</em> of the data. The contents of the first file written from each job represent the first 1/<em>K</em> of the final shuffled data, but it still needs to be shuffled. It’s the same for the second file, and so on. In one pass, we have reduced one shuffle of all the data to <em>N</em> shuffles, each <em>K</em> times smaller. Now, if the shuffles fit in memory on a single machine, we can shuffle it and we’re done. If they don’t fit, we can just run another pass.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Let’s say each job can keep 100GB of data in memory, and we write one hundred 1GB files. Each pass reduces the size of the shuffles needed by 100 times. One pass can shuffle 100GB of data, two passes can shuffle 10TB, three passes 1PB, four passes 100PB, and so on.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Since we implemented this approach, we’ve stopped thinking about shuffling. Now it’s something that happens quickly, without issues. There are certainly better approaches and faster implementations than ours. But this approach solves our bottleneck, and frees us up to tackle the next problem.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="engineering-problem-2-feature-visualization-pipeline">Engineering Problem 2: Feature Visualization Pipeline</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Another engineering challenge has been generating the underlying data for our feature visualizations, which allow users to see specific tokens that are most strongly activated as part of individual features, along with other information (<a id="base-ui-_R_1uedrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://transformer-circuits.pub/2023/monosemantic-features/vis/a1.html" rel="noopener noreferrer">see the Feature Browser from the Towards Monosemanticity paper at this link</a>). </p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">For each feature, we want to find a variety of dataset examples that activate it to different levels, exploring its full distribution. Doing this efficiently for millions of features is an interesting distributed systems problem. Originally, all of this ran in a single job – but we quickly scaled beyond that. Below is a sketch of our current approach.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Our dataset for visualization is 100M tokens, and we need to handle millions of features. First we “shard” over the dataset and features, splitting them into many different parts. Each job iterates over its slice of the dataset and, for its slice of features, keeps track of the <em>K</em> highest activating tokens for each feature and 10*<em>K</em> random tokens that activate the feature (we have already cached the transformer activations in s3, so we don’t need to recompute them). </p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Next, we shard over the features and aggregate the results from the previous pass. This gives us the highest-activating tokens for each feature across the entire dataset, as well as a random set of tokens that activate the feature. These are the examples we’ll show in the feature visualization.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">For each of these examples, we need to calculate how the feature fires on surrounding tokens. Our first approach sharded over features. Each job loads the transformer activations for the examples of the features for which it’s responsible. The problem is that these examples are randomly distributed across the dataset: there’s no easy way to read <em>only</em> the data the job needs. </p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">To improve this, we added a pass sharded over the dataset. In this setup, each job handles a slice of transformer activations, and saves the activations needed for each group of features to a separate file. Then, we can run a pass over features and have easy access to just the data we need. We compute how much the feature fires on surrounding tokens, then write out all the relevant data in a format our frontend website can read and display.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="what-were-looking-for">What we’re looking for</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">When we started working on Sparse Autoencoders, we didn’t know if the approach would work. The more experiments we ran, the more confident we became in our research. That led us to invest more in our infrastructure so we could run larger experiments. The process continued all the way to <a id="base-ui-_R_1uldrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://transformer-circuits.pub/2024/scaling-monosemanticity/" rel="noopener noreferrer">Scaling Monosemanticity</a>, our most recent paper.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">That process points to the type of engineering work we do on the Interpretability team — and the fact that we consider research and engineering to be inseparable. Often, our team members will switch back and forth between research and engineering, squeezing more scale out of our current system to launch a new experiment before returning to the research. Since many research ideas don’t work out, we don’t invest more heavily in their infrastructure until we see success.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Research is a team effort, and it's as much about implementing ideas as it is ruminating on them. We don’t just hypothesize; we test, build, iterate, and scale.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Because of this, we’re particularly interested in hiring generalist engineers who are able to work flexibly across different domains — whether that’s building pipelines, running ML experiments, or optimizing GPU usage.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">If you’re an engineer who fits this bill, and who is passionate about AI safety, we’d love to see your application.
