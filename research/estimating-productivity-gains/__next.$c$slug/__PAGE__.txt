data-component="Headline" id="overview">Overview</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text"><em>What do real conversations with Claude tell us about the effects of AI on labor productivity? Using our privacy-preserving <a href="/research/clio">analysis method</a>, we sample one hundred thousand real conversations from <a id="base-ui-_R_v42drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="http://claude.ai">Claude.ai</a>, estimate how long the tasks in these conversations would take with and without AI assistance, and study the productivity implications across the broader economy. Based on Claude’s estimates, these tasks would take on average about 90 minutes to complete without AI assistance, and Claude speeds up individual tasks by about 80%.</em></p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text"><em>Extrapolating these estimates out suggests <strong>current-generation</strong> AI models could increase US labor productivity growth by 1.8% annually over the next decade</em>—<em>roughly twice the run rate in recent years. But this isn’t a prediction of the future, since we don’t take into account the rate of adoption or the larger productivity effects that would come from much more capable AI systems.</em></p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text"><em>Our analysis has limits. Most notably, we can’t account for additional time humans spend on tasks outside of their conversations with Claude, including validating the quality or accuracy of Claude's work. But as AI models get better at time estimation, we think our methods in this research note could become increasingly useful for understanding how AI is shaping real work.</em></p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text"><em>Here’s a more detailed summary of our results:</em></p><ul class="PA4F1q_reading-column text-body-1-serif post-text"><li><strong>Across one hundred thousand real world conversations, Claude estimates that AI reduces task completion time by 80%.</strong> We use Claude to evaluate anonymized Claude.ai transcripts to estimate the productivity impact of AI. According to Claude’s estimates, people typically use AI for complex tasks that would, on average, take people 1.4 hours to complete. By matching tasks to O*NET occupations and BLS wage data, we estimate these tasks would otherwise cost $55 in human labor.</li><li><strong>The estimated scope, cost, and time savings of tasks varies widely by occupation. </strong>Based on Claude’s estimates, people use Claude for legal and management tasks that would have taken nearly two hours, but for food preparation tasks that would have taken only 30 minutes. And we find that healthcare assistance tasks can be completed 90% more quickly, whereas hardware issues see time savings of 56%. This doesn’t account for the time that humans might spend on these tasks <em>beyond</em> their conversation on <a id="base-ui-_R_3t86drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="http://claude.ai">Claude.ai</a>, however, so we think these estimates might overstate current productivity effects to at least some degree.</li><li><strong>Extrapolating these results to the economy, current generation AI models could increase annual US labor productivity growth by 1.8% over the next decade. This would double the annual growth the US has seen since 2019,</strong> <strong>and places our estimate towards the upper end of <a href="https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2024/11/miracle-or-myth-assessing-the-macroeconomic-productivity-gains-from-artificial-intelligence_fde2a597/b524a072-en.pdf">recent estimates</a>.</strong> Taking as given Claude’s estimates of task-level efficiency gains, we use standard methods to calculate a 1.8% implied annual increase in US labor productivity over the next ten years. However, this estimate does not account for future improvements in AI models (or more sophisticated uses of current technology), which could significantly magnify AI’s economic impact.</li><li><strong>As AI accelerates some tasks, others may become bottlenecks</strong>: We see large speedups for some tasks and much smaller ones in others, even within the same occupational groups. Where AI makes less of a difference, these tasks might become bottlenecks, potentially acting as a constraint on growth.</li></ul><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text"><strong>This gives us a new lens for understanding how AI’s economic impacts over time, which we will track going forward as part of our <a href="/economic-index">Economic Index</a>: </strong>Computing these estimates based on real-world Claude conversations gives us a new lens to understand AI productivity. This complements other approaches, like lab studies in narrow domains, or government statistics which provide more coarse-grained insights. We will track how these estimates change over time to get an evolving picture of these issues as capabilities and adoption continue to progress.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="" loading="lazy" width="9168" height="5160" decoding="async" data-nimg="1" style="color:transparent" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/3777ffd95bdcda140f59.webp"><figcaption class="RMuY0W_root -LJkNq_container -LJkNq_container--default" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><div class="tOQyXa_caption-1 -LJkNq_text" data-component="Caption">An overview of our method and some of our main results. See below for how we validate Claude’s estimates, the assumptions we make, and limitations of our analysis.</div></figcaption></figure></div><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="introduction">Introduction</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">As part of the <a href="/economic-index">Anthropic Economic Index</a>, we have documented how people use Claude across different tasks, industries, and places. We’ve captured the <strong>breadth</strong> of uses—how people use Claude for legal, scientific, and programming tasks—but not their <strong>depth</strong>. How substantial are the tasks for which people use Claude, and how much time does Claude save them?</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">The current version of the Economic Index can't capture this <strong>within-task heterogeneity</strong>—for instance, it can’t distinguish report-writing tasks that take five minutes from those that take five days, or financial modeling tasks that take an afternoon from those that take a few weeks. This makes it difficult to assess AI's economic effects: a software developer might use Claude to write ten pull requests in a day, but if nine are minor documentation updates and one is a critical infrastructure change, simply counting the number of these tasks performed with Claude misses the point.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Not only that, but as model capabilities improve, we want to understand whether they do higher-value work. To understand how AI is reshaping work and productivity, we need to know not just <em>which</em> tasks Claude handles, but <em>how substantial</em> those tasks and time savings are.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Several groups have begun conducting randomized controlled trials to measure productivity gains in narrow domains, including <a id="base-ui-_R_u8ddrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06590">software</a> <a id="base-ui-_R_ugddrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4945566">engineering</a> <a id="base-ui-_R_uoddrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/">tasks</a>, <a id="base-ui-_R_v0ddrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adh2586">writing</a>, and <a id="base-ui-_R_v8ddrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161">customer service</a>. METR's work on <a id="base-ui-_R_vgddrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://metr.org/blog/2025-03-19-measuring-ai-ability-to-complete-long-tasks/">measuring AI’s ability to complete long tasks</a> has demonstrated that AI systems can independently tackle extended, multi-step challenges. But these evaluations consider a narrow set of problems, rather than broad real-world use. To assess AI’s <em>overall</em> impact on the economy, we need a way to analyze hundreds or thousands of real-world AI applications.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">This report takes a first step toward that goal. It uses Claude to estimate how much time it would take a human to complete the tasks that Claude handles, compares that to how long Claude and the human took together, and thereby calculates how much time the AI has saved. While AI models lack context about users' expertise, workflows, and constraints, we find that model-estimated times show promising accuracy for a dataset of software engineering tasks, relative to both human-estimated completion times and time-tracked outcomes.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">In what follows, we present our methodology for estimating task-level time savings, validate our approach against ground-truth data, and then use these estimates to assess which tasks and occupations show the largest productivity gains from AI. We then explore what our task-level estimates imply about aggregate productivity as AI begins to be adopted throughout the economy.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="estimating-task-length-and-time-savings">Estimating task length and time savings</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Using our <a href="/research/clio">privacy-preserving analysis system</a>, we analyzed 100,000 conversation transcripts from Claude.ai (Free, Pro, and Max tiers) to measure the length and time savings of tasks Claude handles. We generated two core estimates for each task:</p><ul class="PA4F1q_reading-column text-body-1-serif post-text"><li><strong>Time estimate without AI</strong>: The hours a human professional would need to complete the task without AI assistance</li><li><strong>Time estimate with AI: </strong>The amount of time it took to complete the task with AI assistance</li></ul><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We used Claude to generate these estimates for each conversation. Following our <a href="/news/the-anthropic-economic-index">Economic Index methodology</a>, we then aggregated these individual chat conversations to tasks in the <a id="base-ui-_R_fgjdrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://www.onetcenter.org/database.html">O*NET</a> taxonomy by taking the median of time estimates for each task. This allowed us to explore how such time estimates vary across tasks and occupations within the economy. Classification prompts are in the Appendix.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Analyzing real-world transcripts enables us to account for <em>intra-task variation</em>. For instance, even if the overall share of <em>designing manufacturing equipment</em> tasks stays fixed, transcript-level information lets us see whether people tackle more complex, longer-timescale projects (or attain greater time savings) with AI over time. Our <a href="/economic-index">Economic Index </a>will track how these estimates evolve over time, and share aggregate datasets that researchers can use to make their own forecasts and conclusions.</p><h3 class="tOQyXa_headline-5 PA4F1q_reading-column post-section" data-component="Headline" id="validation">Validation</h3><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Estimating task duration is <a href="https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/biases/67_J_Personality_and_Social_Psychology_366,_1994.pdf">notoriously difficult for humans</a>. AI models have an even more difficult job, since they lack crucial context about the broader context of tasks (though we expect this context to increase over time as features like <a href="/news/memory">memory</a> and <a href="/news/claude-and-slack">external integrations</a> become more comprehensive). To assess whether Claude’s estimates are informative, we conducted two validation analyses.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text"><strong>Self-consistency testing:</strong> First, we assess whether Claude produces stable estimates of task lengths across different conversation samples, or across variations in our prompts.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We create multiple prompt variations—for example, asking about an "employee with appropriate skills" versus a "skilled professional"—to assess how sensitive estimates are to the way the prompt is phrased. We analyze 1,800 conversations with each variant, where users consented to share these conversations with us, and computed correlations across prompt variants. The results showed strong self-agreement, with log-scale correlations of <em>r</em>=0.89–0.93 across variants.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="" loading="lazy" width="9168" height="5164" decoding="async" data-nimg="1" style="color:transparent" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/f58918c107e8674fcb79.webp"><figcaption class="RMuY0W_root -LJkNq_container -LJkNq_container--default" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><div class="tOQyXa_caption-1 -LJkNq_text" data-component="Caption"><strong>Claude’s estimated human completion times show high correlation across prompt variations.</strong> Prompt 1 asks Claude to estimate the time it would take an "employee with appropriate skills" to complete and Prompt 2 asks about a “human worker” who is “competent in the relevant field.” The two prompts show a log-scale correlation of 0.89, indicating high agreement. Analysis performed on <a href="http://claude.ai">Claude.ai</a> transcripts where users have consented to share them with us for research purposes.</div></figcaption></figure></div><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text"><strong>External benchmarking:</strong> Self-agreement doesn’t matter much if a model’s predictions don’t correspond well to reality. To check this, we tested Claude's time estimation capabilities against a dataset of thousands of real-world <a id="base-ui-_R_fcqdrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://zenodo.org/records/7022735">software development tasks</a> gathered from JIRA tickets for open-source repositories, with both developer estimates and actual tracked completion times.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">This is a very challenging task for Claude, given that Claude receives only the title and description of the JIRA tickets, while the human developers have full context on the codebase and the ticket, and have seen how long similar tasks take to complete. On a subset of 1000 tasks from this benchmark:</p><ul class="PA4F1q_reading-column text-body-1-serif post-text"><li>Human developers themselves achieved ρ=0.50 Spearman correlation with actual times, and a Pearson correlation of r_log=0.67 on the log values, indicating a moderate-strength correlation (higher is better for both values).</li><li>Claude Sonnet 4.5 achieved ρ=0.44 and r_log=0.46</li><li>Claude Sonnet 4.5 with ten examples of tasks and their ground-truth time lengths showed a worse ρ=0.39, but improved r_log=0.48</li></ul><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">This analysis suggests that Claude’s estimates provide <em>directional</em> information that is only slightly worse than software developers’ own estimates. However, we observe that Claude’s estimates are much more compressed than humans—predicting comparatively long times for shorter tasks, and vice versa—and are overall more prone to overestimates. This suggests that the actual differences in task lengths across tasks may be larger than we report, and that actual task lengths may be slightly shorter. Overall, these findings demonstrate that model predictions have meaningful correlation with real-world outcomes, at least in this domain, making them useful for comparing one task to another or tracking changes over time. We also observe higher correlation from Claude Sonnet 4.5 compared to Claude Sonnet 4, suggesting that these estimates may continue to improve with model capabilities.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="" loading="lazy" width="2762" height="1020" decoding="async" data-nimg="1" style="color:transparent" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/2a4d4e791163f1a2a304.webp"><figcaption class="RMuY0W_root -LJkNq_container -LJkNq_container--default" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><div class="tOQyXa_caption-1 -LJkNq_text" data-component="Caption">Correlation of actual time spent on software engineering tasks with developer and Claude estimates. Error bars are 95% CIs per bin.</div></figcaption></figure></div><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="results">Results</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We first use the methods above to estimate task-level savings, then aggregate these into estimates of economy-wide effects.</p><h3 class="tOQyXa_headline-5 PA4F1q_reading-column post-section" data-component="Headline" id="task-level-savings">Task-level savings</h3><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="" loading="lazy" width="9168" height="7336" decoding="async" data-nimg="1" style="color:transparent" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/089ac4e432202c926a85.webp"><figcaption class="RMuY0W_root -LJkNq_container -LJkNq_container--default" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><div class="tOQyXa_caption-1 -LJkNq_text" data-component="Caption">Claude’s estimated task time, average hourly wage of the occupation, implied task cost, and time savings for nine different tasks. <strong>Task time </strong>is estimated by having Claude predict how long a professional would take to perform the task without AI assistance. <strong>Hourly wage</strong> is derived from the Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics (OEWS) May 2024 data. <strong>Task cost</strong> is computed by multiplying the task time by the hourly wage. <strong>Time savings</strong> is computed by estimating the time the human took to complete the task and computing <em>1 - time_with_ai / time_without_ai</em>.</div></figcaption></figure></div><h4 class="tOQyXa_headline-6 PA4F1q_reading-column post-subsection" data-component="Headline" id="example-tasks-demonstrate-a-range-of-time-savings">Example tasks demonstrate a range of time savings</h4><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Looking at individual tasks within occupations provides concrete examples of where and how AI might be delivering time savings. At the most extreme end, we see users complete curriculum development tasks that Claude thinks would take 4.5 hours in just 11 minutes. Such tasks have an implied labor cost of $115 based on the average hourly wage of teachers.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">People also use AI to save 87% of the time it would take to write invoices, memos, and other documents (at least for the type of documents Claude is asked to handle). Finally, AI saves 80% of time on financial analyst tasks like interpreting financial data for tasks that would ordinarily cost $31 in wages.</p><h4 class="tOQyXa_headline-6 PA4F1q_reading-column post-subsection" data-component="Headline" id="task-length-varies-dramatically-across-occupations">Task length varies dramatically across occupations</h4><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Human time estimates show that Claude handles tasks of very different lengths depending on the occupation. In the below plots, we show averages for each occupation category among the subset of tasks that Claude is used for<sup class="caption PA4F1q_sup PA4F1q_footnote-marker"><a href="#footnote-1" id="footnote-ref-1" class="PA4F1q_footnote-ref" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup>. The average management task where Claude is used (e.g. selecting investments) is estimated to take humans 2.0 hours to complete, followed by legal (1.8 hours), education (1.7), and arts/media tasks (1.6). At the other end of the spectrum, food preparation tasks (e.g. planning or pricing menu items), installation/maintenance, and transportation tasks all take 0.3-0.5hrs on average, suggesting more circumscribed tasks, or tasks with less waiting time. Given that Claude’s time estimates tend to underestimate long tasks and overestimate short tasks, it is possible that these differences might be even greater in practice.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="" loading="lazy" width="9168" height="5160" decoding="async" data-nimg="1" style="color:transparent" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/70b95883577159a57c32.webp"><figcaption class="RMuY0W_root -LJkNq_container -LJkNq_container--default" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><div class="tOQyXa_caption-1 -LJkNq_text" data-component="Caption">Various figures derived from Claude’s time estimates for SOC major groups. The average management task would cost $133 for a professional compared to $119 for legal tasks and $8 for tasks relating to food preparation and serving. Business and financial tasks average $69 while computer and mathematical tasks average $82.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Across all tasks we observe, we estimate Claude handles work that would cost a median of $54 in professional labor to hire an expert to perform the work in each conversation. Of course, the actual performance of current models will likely be worse than a human expert for many tasks, though recent research suggests <a id="base-ui-_R_7padrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://openai.com/index/gdpval/">the gap is closing</a> across a wide range of different applications.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Across major occupational groups, we observe a positive correlation between average hourly wage among tasks/occupations in our sample and the human-time-equivalent duration of the tasks Claude is asked to handle. For example, the Management and Legal occupational categories rank at the top of the classification in terms of average hourly wage—aligning with Claude’s strengths in complex knowledge work.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="" loading="lazy" width="9168" height="5160" decoding="async" data-nimg="1" style="color:transparent" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/000241fc072b3059088e.png"><figcaption class="RMuY0W_root -LJkNq_container -LJkNq_container--default" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><div class="tOQyXa_caption-1 -LJkNq_text" data-component="Caption">Correlation between average hourly wage of an occupation category and the average Claude-estimated task duration in our sample. Higher-wage occupations categories (e.g. Management and Legal) have tasks with more complex usage in our sample (<em>r</em>=0.8).</div></figcaption></figure></div><h4 class="tOQyXa_headline-6 PA4F1q_reading-column post-subsection" data-component="Headline" id="time-savings-are-highly-uneven-across-occupations">Time savings are highly uneven across occupations</h4><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Our human time and cost estimates capture the <em>magnitude</em> of tasks people tackle with AI. But the time <em>savings</em>—Claude’s estimate for how much faster work gets done with AI—reflects the productivity gains that might come from using AI for those tasks.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">The median conversation experienced an estimated 84% time savings, though we see considerable variation across tasks and categories. For example, the task of checking diagnostic images only shows 20% time savings, likely because this is already a task that can be done quickly by experts without AI assistance. By contrast, the task of compiling information from reports sees approximately 95% time savings, likely because AI systems can read, extract, and cite information much more quickly than people. Overall, the distribution of time saved by task is concentrated within the 50-95% range, peaking between 80-90%.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">These large time savings align with Claude’s abilities to read and write far faster than people can. However, our approach doesn’t take into account the additional work people need to do to refine Claude’s outputs to a finished state, or whether they continue iterating on the work product across multiple sessions—both of which would result in smaller time savings. Past randomized controlled trials have typically found smaller time savings, including <a id="base-ui-_R_u9gdrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06590">56%</a>, <a id="base-ui-_R_uhgdrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adh2586">40%</a>, <a id="base-ui-_R_upgdrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4945566">26%</a>, <a id="base-ui-_R_v1gdrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161">14%</a> and even <a id="base-ui-_R_v9gdrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/">negative</a> time savings across different applications—perhaps due to these effects or because these studies examined earlier generations of models.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="" loading="lazy" width="2292" height="1291" decoding="async" data-nimg="1" style="color:transparent" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/14cfe32697a8cb13a19a.png"><figcaption class="RMuY0W_root -LJkNq_container -LJkNq_container--default" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><div class="tOQyXa_caption-1 -LJkNq_text" data-component="Caption">Density plot of time savings across O*NET tasks in our sample. We see that Claude’s estimated time savings are uneven across tasks in our sample, with most falling between 50 and 95%. The overall median savings is 81%. Time savings are computed by <em>1 - time_with_ai / time_without_ai</em>. Our estimates do not take into account the time spent refining Claude’s output outside of the chat window.</div></figcaption></figure></div><h3 class="tOQyXa_headline-5 PA4F1q_reading-column post-section" data-component="Headline" id="from-task-level-efficiency-gains-to-economy-wide-productivity-effects">From task-level efficiency gains to economy-wide productivity effects</h3><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">The above estimates capture AI-driven productivity gains at the task level. To understand macro-level impacts, this section models how these gains could aggregate across the entire economy, assuming they play out according to Claude’s estimates.</p><h4 class="tOQyXa_headline-6 PA4F1q_reading-column post-subsection" data-component="Headline" id="methodology">Methodology</h4><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">To estimate economy-wide productivity effects, we use Hulten’s theorem, a standard method that allows us to aggregate efficiency gains at the task-level to the broader US economy<sup class="caption PA4F1q_sup PA4F1q_footnote-marker"><a href="#footnote-2" id="footnote-ref-2" class="PA4F1q_footnote-ref" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup>. As in <a href="https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2024-04/The%20Simple%20Macroeconomics%20of%20AI.pdf">Acemoglu (2024)</a>’s “baseline” approach, we model the implied increase in labor productivity as a weighted average over task-level productivity gains—a modeling choice that implicitly assumes that capital investment will increase as a result of an increase in total factor productivity (TFP) associated with AI adoption. In this framework, the implied increase TFP is the gain in labor productivity multiplied by the labor share of income<sup class="caption PA4F1q_sup PA4F1q_footnote-marker"><a href="#footnote-3" id="footnote-ref-3" class="PA4F1q_footnote-ref" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Footnote 3">3</a></sup>.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text"><strong>Task composition</strong>: For each occupation, we obtain a list of work tasks from O*NET. We then use Claude to estimate what fraction of workers' time is spent on each of those tasks. For example, Claude estimates that programmers spend 23% of their time writing and maintaining code, 15% analyzing and rewriting programs, and smaller fractions on testing, documentation, and meetings.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text"><strong>Task-level productivity improvements</strong>: In the previous section, we provided estimates we can use to compute how much more quickly each task is completed with AI assistance.<strong> </strong>We take the log difference between time without AI and the time <em>with</em> AI to generate a productivity improvement value, and conservatively assign tasks not observed in our sample a null improvement.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text"><strong>Economy-wide estimate</strong>: We weight each task's implied productivity gains by its economic importance using two factors: (i) the fraction of time that Claude estimates the occupation spends on that task (as above), and (ii) the occupation's share of the total US wage bill (the number of people employed in that occupational category multiplied by the average wage, then divided by total wage bill across all occupations). For the total wage bill, we use May 2024 OEWS data. This approach implicitly assumes the time estimates that Claude produces represent reliable averages across all instances of each task, and that Claude or similar AI systems will be adopted across the entire US economy.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="" loading="lazy" width="9168" height="5164" decoding="async" data-nimg="1" style="color:transparent" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/75a99d62598d9a4f1f3f.png"><figcaption class="RMuY0W_root -LJkNq_container -LJkNq_container--default" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><div class="tOQyXa_caption-1 -LJkNq_text" data-component="Caption"><strong>US economy-wide labor productivity impact: top ten occupations.</strong> Overall, Claude’s estimates imply a 1.8% annualized increase (dotted line) in US labor productivity assuming current AI systems were adopted universally for all tasks we observe, driven by software, management, marketing, and customer service tasks. This corresponds to an implied 1.08% annualized increase in TFP. The average <em>ln(time estimate ratio)</em> represents the time-weighted productivity gain across all tasks in each occupation, where <em>time estimate ratio = time with AI / time without AI</em>. Labor statistics derived from OEWS 2024 data.</div></figcaption></figure></div><h3 class="tOQyXa_headline-5 PA4F1q_reading-column post-section" data-component="Headline" id="findings">Findings</h3><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Assuming 10 years for AI to reach universal adoption across the US economy—and using current models—we calculate that Claude’s estimates imply an annual increase in US labor productivity of 1.8%. This would nearly double the current long-term growth rate, which has averaged 2.1% per year since 1947 and 1.8% since 2019. Assuming that labor’s share of total factor productivity is 0.6<sup class="caption PA4F1q_sup PA4F1q_footnote-marker"><a href="#footnote-4" id="footnote-ref-4" class="PA4F1q_footnote-ref" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Footnote 4">4</a></sup>, this implies an overall total factor productivity increase of 1.1% per year. Given that TFP growth has tended to be less than 1% since the early 2000s, these estimates suggest that, even broad deployment of current AI systems could cause growth to double: achieving the rates of the late 1990s, and of the 1960s and 1970s<sup class="caption PA4F1q_sup PA4F1q_footnote-marker"><a href="#footnote-5" id="footnote-ref-5" class="PA4F1q_footnote-ref" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Footnote 5">5</a></sup>.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">This estimated increase in aggregate labor productivity implied by task-level efficiency gains is within the range of recent estimates of AI’s potential impact on productivity, though it lies towards the upper end (Filippucci, Gal, and Schief, 2024).</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Importantly, this exercise assumes that AI capabilities (and humans’ effectiveness in using AI) remain the same over the next 10 years as when we took our sample. This, though, seems unlikely to hold: we think that AI will <a href="/news/core-views-on-ai-safety">continue to improve rapidly</a> over the coming years.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Therefore, this estimate should be taken as an exercise exploring what might happen based on <em>current usage patterns,</em> not a prediction of the impact on productivity that is actually most likely to happen. As we have written about in other work, we remain extremely alert to the possibility that AI causes significant labor market disruptions, which would likely be associated with larger increases in productivity due to AI. As models progress, this could represent an approximate lower bound on the productivity effects of AI, although our estimate does not account for unevenness in adoption, which might reduce real-world productivity gains in the short term.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="" loading="lazy" width="9168" height="5160" decoding="async" data-nimg="1" style="color:transparent" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/b3f91cf1c10e3b4100ff.webp"><figcaption class="RMuY0W_root -LJkNq_container -LJkNq_container--default" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><div class="tOQyXa_caption-1 -LJkNq_text" data-component="Caption">Labor productivity growth in the nonfarm business sector. The chart shows five year moving averages of the year-over-year percent change in labor productivity. We see a general decline from almost 3% in the 1960s to around 1.5% the last few years.</div></figcaption></figure></div><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Reflecting the fact that <a href="https://assets.anthropic.com/m/218c82b858610fac/original/Economic-Index.pdf">some tasks and occupations appear much more frequently in our data than others</a>, we observe similar phenomena in occupations’ contributions to labor productivity as well.<strong> </strong>Software developers contribute most (19%) to the total labor productivity gain attributable to AI. General and Operations Managers (about 6%), Market Research Analysts and Marketing Specialists (5%), Customer Service Representatives (4%) and Secondary School Teachers (3%) round out the top five.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">In contrast, restaurants, healthcare delivery, construction, and retail contribute much less to the overall productivity effect. This is mostly because few of their tasks appear in our data—largely because these occupations have few associated tasks in our sample.</p><h3 class="tOQyXa_headline-5 PA4F1q_reading-column post-section" data-component="Headline" id="how-might-ai-change-how-workers-spend-their-time">How might AI change how workers spend their time?</h3><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">If workers are able to accelerate a subset of their occupational tasks with AI, the tasks where AI provides less speedup may come to represent a larger and thus more important share of those occupations’ work. For example, AI might help a home inspector prepare reports, but if the inspector still has to spend the same amount of time physically traveling to the property to perform the inspection in person, this could make inspections a greater fraction of the job overall.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">The figure below illustrates this for a few occupations. For software developers, AI speeds up the process of software development, testing, documentation, and manipulating data. But we do not currently see meaningful AI use for coordinating system installation or supervising the work of other technologists or engineers. For teachers, we see that AI assists with lesson and activity planning, but not with sponsoring extracurricular clubs or enforcing rules in the classroom.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">From a growth perspective, these observations align well with a recent observation from <a id="base-ui-_R_7q5drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://www.nber.org/papers/w23928">Aghion, Jones, and Jones</a>: “growth may be constrained not by what we are good at but rather by what is essential and yet hard to improve.”</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="" loading="lazy" width="9168" height="8096" decoding="async" data-nimg="1" style="color:transparent" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/860539f54b2d9fa3e6f5.png"><figcaption class="RMuY0W_root -LJkNq_container -LJkNq_container--default" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><div class="tOQyXa_caption-1 -LJkNq_text" data-component="Caption">Four different occupations along with “accelerated” tasks that show large potential time savings, and potential “bottleneck” tasks that do not appear in our sample. For example, software engineers see large estimated time savings in developing and debugging software, but not in supervising programmers. Weekly time fractions are estimated by Claude (see previous section).</div></figcaption></figure></div><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="limitations">Limitations</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Our approach has several limitations that we think warrant further research on this topic:</p><ul class="PA4F1q_reading-column text-body-1-serif post-text"><li><strong>Claude’s predictions are imperfect and we lack real-world validation of Claude’s time estimates</strong>: AI systems are imperfect predictors, and can’t see activity that happens after the user finishes their interaction with the model. While we expect these estimates will improve with models capabilities, using model estimates introduces a significant source of noise. While our estimates show that models are approaching human performance at estimating task times, and humans are far from perfect themselves, we lack real-world data to validate the estimates that Claude provides.</li><li><strong>Task taxonomy limitations</strong>: Real jobs are more complex than an O*NET task list, and the time allocations we estimate for each task are only approximate. Many important aspects of work—tacit knowledge, relationships, judgment under uncertainty—don't appear in these formal task descriptions, and the connections <em>between</em> tasks may matter just as much or more to productivity as the time savings for those tasks in isolation. While we show large predicted time savings for individual tasks, <a id="base-ui-_R_3ta9drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/">a recent randomized controlled trial</a> studying end-to-end software features did not see time savings due to AI.</li><li><strong>Structural assumptions: </strong>In our calculations above, we compare the time it would take a professional to complete a given task without AI to the time it took with AI. But this could either <em>understate</em> the productivity gains – since it takes additional resources we’re not accounting for to hire an employee and communicate context, and possibly overstate it, if the quality of the AI’s work is worse than a human’s.</li><li><strong>Restructuring of organizations:</strong> Historically, the largest productivity gains for individual firms have followed from <a id="base-ui-_R_3ri9drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/2006600">restructuring business operations</a> to adopt new technologies. Our model can help predict the <em>effects</em> of such a restructuring, but it cannot predict how companies might decide to restructure, or how quickly this process might happen.</li><li><strong>The role of innovation:</strong> Technological innovation is <a id="base-ui-_R_3rm9drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/1926047">the engine</a> of economic growth. Our model does not capture how AI systems could accelerate or even automate the scientific process, nor the effects that would have on productivity, growth, and the structure of work.</li><li><strong>Limited data</strong>: Our dataset is derived from <a id="base-ui-_R_3rq9drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="http://claude.ai">Claude.ai</a> conversations only. This sample is not representative of the full spectrum of AI uses, and there’s likely some selection effect where the instances of tasks people use Claude for are the ones they think Claude will be most useful. Additionally, due to our finite sample size, we likely miss some less common AI tasks.</li></ul><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">The measurement infrastructure we develop here enables continuous tracking of the effect of AI on time savings at large scale. As models improve and better methods address these limitations, we can re-estimate these time savings and identify how these capability improvements translate into broader economic impacts. We expect to track these changes in the months and years ahead.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Claude handles tasks of widely varying complexity—from simple food preparation questions that would take a few minutes to complete, to complex legal and management tasks that would take multiple hours. But what is the aggregate effect of this work?</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Based on Claude’s time estimates per task (and assuming universal adoption over the next 10 years), we find that use of <em>current</em> models implies a potential increase in US labor productivity of 1.8% per year—a doubling of the recent rate of labor productivity growth. Based on current AI use, these gains would be concentrated in technology, education, and professional services, while retail, restaurants, and transportation sectors would see minimal impact. We’ll be tracking these changes over time as part of our <a href="/economic-index">Economic Index</a> as model capabilities, products, and adoption continue to progress.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">These productivity gains come from making existing tasks faster to complete. Historically, though, transformative productivity improvements—from electrification, computing, or the internet—came not from speeding up old tasks, but from fundamentally reorganizing production. In futures like these, AI not only makes implementing features faster, but companies restructure meetings and code review to validate and ship those features faster, whether using AI or through other means.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Our framework could be used to help estimate the effects of such restructuring, but it cannot predict which changes will occur, or how quickly. An important direction for future work is understanding this question—to get a better understanding of when and how firms are reorganizing themselves around emerging AI capabilities. The answer will determine when AI makes the jump from providing significant but bounded productivity boosts, to representing the kind of structural transformation that has historically defined technological revolutions.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="appendix">Appendix</h2><h3 class="tOQyXa_headline-5 PA4F1q_reading-column post-section" data-component="Headline" id="comparison-of-claudes-estimates-to-other-estimates">Comparison of Claude’s estimates to other estimates</h3><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="" loading="lazy" width="9168" height="5164" decoding="async" data-nimg="1" style="color:transparent" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/eb68cb6a5a141430d93f.webp"><figcaption class="RMuY0W_root -LJkNq_container -LJkNq_container--default" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><div class="tOQyXa_caption-1 -LJkNq_text" data-component="Caption">Predicted increase in annual labor productivity growth over a 10-year horizon due to AI.
