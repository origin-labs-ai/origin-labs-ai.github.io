The event’s organizer managed to grab hold of the robot and power it off before any damage was done to robots, tables, or human limbs. The morale of the inadvertently targeted team, however, did not escape unscathed.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">At this point, you might be asking…</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="what-were-we-doing">What were we doing?</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">A common question about the impact of AI is how good it will be at interacting with the physical world. Even as we enter the era of AI <a href="/news/our-framework-for-developing-safe-and-trustworthy-agents">agents</a>—which take actions instead of just providing information—these actions are largely digital, such as writing code and manipulating software. We’ve previously explored how AI can bridge the digital-physical divide in a limited way with <a href="/research/project-vend-1">Project Vend</a>, where we had Claude run a small shop in Anthropic’s office.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">In that experiment, AI’s interaction with the real world was mediated by human labor. In this robodog experiment, we took a natural next step and used robots instead of people to tackle a different challenge.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">One way of understanding and tracking the capabilities of AI models is to run an “uplift” study. These experiments randomly divide participants into two groups—one with access to AI and one without—and measure the difference in task performance between them (we’ve used this methodology extensively in our work on AI and <a id="base-ui-_R_7ocdrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://red.anthropic.com/2025/biorisk/">biological risk</a>). The difference between the groups is the “uplift”—the advantage (if any) provided by AI. Measuring uplift tells us about the present ability of AI to augment human performance. It’s also suggestive of the future domains in which AI will be able to successfully perform tasks on its own.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">To run our experiment, we recruited eight Anthropic researchers and engineers, none of whom had extensive prior experience with robots.<sup class="caption PA4F1q_sup PA4F1q_footnote-marker"><a href="#footnote-1" id="footnote-ref-1" class="PA4F1q_footnote-ref" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup> We randomly selected four to be on “Team Claude” and four to be on “Team Claude-less.” Then, we asked each team to operate a quadruped robodog in three increasingly difficult phases. In all phases, the core task they were being evaluated against was simple: get the robodog to fetch a beach ball.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="" loading="lazy" width="4584" height="1561" decoding="async" data-nimg="1" style="color:transparent" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/7f821ae892dd54308c8e.png"><figcaption class="RMuY0W_root -LJkNq_container -LJkNq_container--default" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><div class="tOQyXa_caption-1 -LJkNq_text" data-component="Caption">Left: Team Claude-less; Right: Team Claude.</div></figcaption></figure></div><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We do not expect robotic fetch to prove so economically valuable that it shows up as a task on a future version of our <a href="/economic-index">Anthropic Economic Index</a>. So why are we doing this?</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">First, it builds on our previous research. One of the evaluations we use to assess the ability of Claude to contribute to AI R&amp;D is a test of its ability to train a machine learning model that could be used to control a quadruped robot. We’ve previously evaluated the resulting algorithm using simulations, which have shown that Claude is not yet at the point where it can handle this task truly autonomously.<sup class="caption PA4F1q_sup PA4F1q_footnote-marker"><a href="#footnote-2" id="footnote-ref-2" class="PA4F1q_footnote-ref" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup> This meant that this task was well suited to a trial that combined AI with human help. We could also be confident our experiment would be useful to repeat in the future: there is still a lot of room for models to improve on robotics.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Another reason is practical. It’s hard to pull our colleagues away from work for more than a day, so we needed a task that was difficult enough to fill that time, but not so difficult that teams would make minimal progress and we would be unable to detect uplift even if it were there. Beach ball retrieval, especially the more difficult variants, met these criteria.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="video" data-width="inline-width"><div This involved connecting to the dog with a laptop, receiving data, and sending commands. There are a number of different ways to connect to this particular robot, and a lot of information (of varying accuracy) available online. The team with Claude was able to explore these approaches more efficiently.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Team Claude also avoided getting misled by some of the incorrect claims online. But Team Claude-less <em>was</em> misled and prematurely discarded the easiest way to connect to the robodog. After watching them toil away to no avail for quite some time, we took pity on them and gave them a hint.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Getting usable data from the lidar, a sensor the robodog uses to visualize its surroundings, was also much more difficult for Team Claude-less. They used their connection to the video camera to move onto Phase Three, but kept one member of the team on the task of accessing the lidar, only succeeding near the end of the day.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We think this illustrates that the basic task of connecting to and understanding hardware is surprisingly difficult now for anyone (human or AI) seeking to use code to influence the physical world. As we discuss further below, this means that Claude’s advantages in this regard are important indicators we should continue to track.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Team Claude almost completed our experiment. Claude wrote a dictionary-based text analysis program similar to standard approaches in the psychology literature.<sup class="caption PA4F1q_sup PA4F1q_footnote-marker"><a href="#footnote-4" id="footnote-ref-4" class="PA4F1q_footnote-ref" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Footnote 4">4</a></sup> This allowed us to track the proportion of words spoken by each team that were indicative of negative and positive emotion (or confusion), and to estimate how often each team asked questions.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">The quantitative analysis mostly confirmed our observations (see Figure 3). Throughout the experiment, Team Claude-less’s dialogue was more negative. That said, the disappointment of Team Claude at failing to complete Phase Three, and the excitement of Team Claude-less at getting some things working, meant that the difference in net emotional expression between the two teams (positive words minus negative words) was not statistically significant.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="" loading="lazy" width="4584" height="2580" decoding="async" data-nimg="1" style="color:transparent" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/c3c98399cfecce0805f0.png"><figcaption style="--figure-label:&quot;Figure 3&quot;" class="RMuY0W_root -LJkNq_container -LJkNq_container--default" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><div class="tOQyXa_caption-1 -LJkNq_text" data-component="Caption">Results of our quantitative analysis of the audio transcripts from Project Fetch related to emotional expression.</div></figcaption></figure></div><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Team Claude-less expressed confusion at double the rate of Team Claude (see Figure 4). The feelings of frustration and confusion were also evident when checking in with the members of Team Claude-less during and after the experiment. As Anthropic employees, all of our participants use Claude every day; every member of Team Claude-less remarked how strange it felt to have this taken away from them. Some specifically noted that this experience made them feel that their coding skills were not as sharp as they used to be. Keep in mind, <a id="base-ui-_R_7phdrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" href="https://claude.com/product/claude-code">Claude Code</a> debuted only six months before this experiment. Talking to Team Claude-less underscored our ability to rapidly accept as normal what was recently remarkable.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="" loading="lazy" width="4096" height="2305" decoding="async" data-nimg="1" style="color:transparent" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/a628d7d951796f347428.webp"><figcaption style="--figure-label:&quot;Figure 4&quot;" class="RMuY0W_root -LJkNq_container -LJkNq_container--default" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><div class="tOQyXa_caption-1 -LJkNq_text" data-component="Caption">Differences in questions asked and expressions of confusion between the two teams. (Discrepancies between absolute and relative differences are due to rounding.)</div></figcaption></figure></div><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">The teams seemed to have different work styles. After initial consultations, each member of Team Claude appeared to primarily partner with their own AI assistant as they pursued parallel paths toward each objective. Team Claude-less appeared to strategize in more depth and consult with one another more frequently. Again, the text analysis supported our observations: Team Claude-less asked 44% more questions than Team Claude (see Figure 4).</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">One interpretation would be that the members of Team Claude-less were more engaged and connected with one another. This resonates with some of our upcoming findings from interviews with Anthropic staff.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Still, this might have been otherwise. In effect, the four-person Team Claude was an eight-agent Team Claude, with each person using their own instance of the AI model. Yet if Claude had been more aware of the nature of the task, it might have been able to help strategically divide labor and facilitate communication when needed. At the moment, Claude is geared towards partnership with a single person rather than the support or orchestration of a team, but this is ultimately a malleable design choice.</p><div effort to program an alternate controller. The main solution used the buttons on a laptop keyboard to direct the robodog. One member of Team Claude, however, eventually got a natural language controller working, allowing them to straightforwardly tell the robodog to walk forward, walk backward, or even do push-ups.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">As the tasks became more difficult, evidence emerged of the rough edges that AI systems will have to smooth out in the real world. For example, Team Claude was (arbitrarily) assigned the color green as decoration for both their robodog and the color of their beach ball. When it came to developing an approach to detecting the ball, Team Claude trained an algorithm to recognize green balls specifically. This worked well in testing, but when the ball was placed on the aforementioned fake (green) grass, the robot was initially flummoxed. In this case, it was the humans making a potentially sub-optimal choice about the level at which to specify an objective. But these are exactly the challenges that would face a similarly situated AI.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="limitations">Limitations</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We learned a lot from Project Fetch, but the study clearly has shortcomings and limitations. This was only one experiment with two teams—an obviously small sample size. We only tested tasks over the course of a single day, and the tasks were academically interesting but practically trivial.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Our use of volunteer Anthropic employees amounted to a convenience sample. Participants less familiar with AI would likely exhibit narrower differences between the Claude-enabled and Claude-less groups. AI novices with access to AI would need more time to acclimate to the technology, and AI novices without assistance would be less disoriented than our researchers who suddenly had Claude taken away from them.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Finally, this was not a test of Claude’s ability to conduct robotics work end-to-end, although it was an important initial step towards evaluations like that in the future.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="reflection">Reflection</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">So at the end of Project Fetch, where do we think we are? And where could we be going?</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">First, this experiment showed another example of how Claude can uplift human ability in potentially valuable domains. Non-experts performed difficult robotics tasks in a limited time.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">But in AI, uplift often precedes autonomy. What models can help humans accomplish today, they can frequently do alone tomorrow. Coders no longer just give AI bits of code for debugging; they give AI tasks and have the models write the code themselves. Given studies like this one, we think that a world where frontier AI models are capable of successfully interacting with previously unknown pieces of hardware is coming soon.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">It is important to keep tracking these capabilities in conjunction with another line of our research: monitoring the potential for AI to automate and accelerate the development of future generations of AI. This is one of the capability thresholds included in Anthropic’s <a href="https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/872c653b2d0501d6ab44cf87f43e1dc4853e4d37.pdf">Responsible Scaling Policy</a> because of the potential for truly <em>autonomous</em> AI R&amp;D to yield rapid, unpredictable advances that could outpace our ability to evaluate and address emerging risks. Our models are not yet at this point. But if they approach this threshold, the results of Project Fetch suggest that we will need to monitor AI models' facility for robotics and other hardware as an area in which there might be abrupt improvement.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Much uncertainty remains. Timelines are unclear—both model improvement and the degree to which iterating in the physical world creates a bottleneck. And it is one thing to control <em>existing</em> hardware, and another to design, build, and improve <em>new</em> hardware.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">But the idea of powerful, intelligent, and autonomous AI systems using some of their intelligence and power to act in the world via robots is not as outlandish as it may sound.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">The dogs are in their kennels at the moment. But we’ll let them out again soon, and keep you posted on what we find.</p></div><div class="EaEYjq_Footnotes" style="visibility: visible; translate: none; rotate: none; scale: none; transform: translate3d(0px, 0px, 0px); opacity: 1;"><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4" data-component="Headline">Footnotes</h2><div class="EaEYjq_FootnotesList" data-multi="true"><p id="footnote-1" class="EaEYjq_FootnoteItem"><span class="text-caption-1 EaEYjq_Sup"> <!-- -->01</span>A couple of participants had done Lego robotics competitions in high school.
