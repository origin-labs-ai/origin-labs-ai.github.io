data-theme="ivory"><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text"><em>We are sharing the first complete computer-checked proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem. Claude worked largely autonomously over 11 days to write the proof in the Lean programming language. Below, we describe how the formalization was done and share some thoughts about what this work could mean for research mathematics.<br><br></em>Around 1637, Pierre de Fermat jotted down a claim in the margin of his copy of Diophantus’s Arithmetica<em> </em>that would become one of the most famous mathematical conjectures of all time: no positive integers a, b, c satisfy aⁿ + bⁿ = cⁿ for any n &gt; 2. <a id="base-ui-_R_fa1drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1BSFyEIY2BY" rel="noopener noreferrer">Fermat’s Last Theorem</a> (FLT), as the conjecture became known, turned out to be incredibly difficult to prove. The first proof, from Sir Andrew Wiles in<em> </em>1995, ran to 129 pages and required months of painstaking work to verify.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">A decade later, Dutch computer scientist Jan Bergstra proposed “formalizing” Wiles’s proof: converting the mathematical reasoning into a form computers can check automatically. Since then, mathematicians have been developing the methods needed to encode such a complex proof, including a multi-year community effort kicked off in 2024 by Kevin Buzzard at Imperial College London <a id="base-ui-_R_7k2drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://lean-lang.org/use-cases/flt/" rel="noopener noreferrer">to complete the formalization</a> using the <a id="base-ui-_R_7o2drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lean_(proof_assistant)" rel="noopener noreferrer">Lean proof assistant</a>.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Recently, Tianyi Peng, an Anthropic researcher whose group at Columbia University builds tools for AI formalization, set out to test whether Claude could make progress on formalizing FLT.<sup class="caption PA4F1q_sup PA4F1q_footnote-marker"><a href="#footnote-1" id="footnote-ref-1" class="PA4F1q_footnote-ref" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup> The result went further than he expected. In 11 days, working largely autonomously, Claude produced the first end-to-end, computer-checked proof of FLT. Along the way, it wrote 13 million lines of Lean and proved 29,500 intermediate theorems.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We shared the <a id="base-ui-_R_3s4drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://github.com/anthropics/fermats-last-theorem" rel="noopener noreferrer">resulting proof</a> with Kevin Buzzard, who said:</p><div class="PA4F1q_reading-column"><blockquote class="PA4F1q_blockquote post-text">This extraordinary autoformalization achievement, which Anthropic researchers say only took 11 days, proves Fermat’s Last Theorem with no assumptions other than the axioms of mathematics. Along the way we see autoformalization of algebra, harmonic analysis, geometry and number theory, and we learn that AI autoformalization artefacts are now robust enough to be built upon; the proof is multi-layered.</blockquote></div><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Automatically formalizing a proof as complex as FLT is a significant step towards a future in which all of mathematics can be readily checked. As AI produces ever more proofs, the ability to easily formalize work can lighten the burden of evaluating new results (a process that can take years). We are hopeful that it will become easier, not harder, to trust the body of knowledge upon which mathematics is built.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="the-challenge-of-verifying-mathematical-proofs">The challenge of verifying mathematical proofs</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Unlike <a href="/research/riemann-zeta">recent AI-driven</a> work on the Riemann hypothesis, which produced novel <em>mathematics</em>, what’s novel here is the <em>verification</em>—checking a mathematical proof as one would check a mathematical computation with a calculator. Proving math theorems requires assembling complex logical chains, and if a single link is broken, everything that follows it might turn out to be false. Understanding a novel result deeply enough to be confident in its correctness can take months, or even years, of work.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Fermat’s Last Theorem is an illustrative example.<sup class="caption PA4F1q_sup PA4F1q_footnote-marker"><a href="#footnote-2" id="footnote-ref-2" class="PA4F1q_footnote-ref" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup> Fermat wrote down the theorem’s statement in the margin of a book, alongside a tantalizing note:</p><div class="PA4F1q_reading-column"><blockquote class="PA4F1q_blockquote post-text">I have discovered a truly marvelous proof of this, which this margin is too narrow to contain.</blockquote></div><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">For over 350 years, generations of mathematicians searched for a proof of FLT, marvelous or otherwise. In 1908, a prize of 100,000 German gold marks (the equivalent of 1–2 million dollars today) was announced for anyone who could produce a correct proof, and 621 <em>incorrect</em> attempts were produced in the first year alone.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">In June 1993, Wiles presented what he believed to be the first correct proof of FLT in a three-day series of lectures. Two months into an intensive verification effort by several mathematicians, a reviewer asked Wiles a question that exposed a critical gap. Wiles spent a year trying to fix it, first alone and then with his former student Richard Taylor. He was on the brink of abandoning the project when he finally realized an approach he’d discarded earlier could fix the proof. <br><br>Wiles published the first correct proof of FLT in May 1995; it relied on modern mathematical techniques that were far beyond what would have been known to Fermat in 1637. Since an elementary proof has not been found after centuries of trying, the mathematical community now believes Fermat’s own original “marvelous proof” <a id="base-ui-_R_7scdrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://mathenchant.wordpress.com/2016/05/16/fermats-last-theorem-the-curious-incident-of-the-boasting-frenchman/" rel="noopener noreferrer">was incorrect</a>.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="formalizing-fermats-last-theorem">Formalizing Fermat’s Last Theorem</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">One way to check a proof’s correctness is to ask a computer to do it. Proof assistants like Lean verify the logic of a proof algorithmically, demonstrating its correctness beyond a doubt. The difficult part for humans is rewriting the proof so Lean can understand it. While a proof written for human readers will skip many obvious steps, Lean needs to see every step, no matter how trivial. Human proofs also build on centuries of published work, while a formalization starts from the tiny fraction of math that’s been formalized already.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">For FLT, the formalization process was expected to take years. Just the <a target="_blank" href="https://imperialcollegelondon.github.io/FLT/blueprint.pdf" rel="noopener noreferrer">blueprint</a> the mathematical community has been using to describe the initial phase of the project runs to 86 pages.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Claude completed the proof in 11 days, producing computer-verifiable proofs of 30,300 theorems along the way (using 29,500 in the final proof). Dozens of Claude agents collaborated to define concepts, prove intermediate theorems, and use those theorems to prove ever harder statements. At 13 million lines of Lean code, Claude’s proof is over 5x the size of Mathlib, the principal community library of mathematical proofs this theorem builds on.<sup class="caption PA4F1q_sup PA4F1q_footnote-marker"><a href="#footnote-3" id="footnote-ref-3" class="PA4F1q_footnote-ref" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Footnote 3">3</a></sup></p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="video" data-width="full-width"><div class="qJNyFq_post-wrapper"><figure class="qJNyFq_post-video-container"><video controls="" playsinline="" muted="" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/78923d5cd2f1e6e7087e.mp4"></video><figcaption class="RMuY0W_root -LJkNq_container -LJkNq_container--default" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><div class="tOQyXa_caption-1 -LJkNq_text" data-component="Caption">Time progression of FLT formalization.</div></figcaption></figure></div></div><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Claude’s proof follows <a target="_blank" href="https://www.math.mcgill.ca/darmon/pub/Articles/Expository/05.DDT/paper.pdf" rel="noopener noreferrer">a simplified version of Wiles’s proof from Darmon, Diamond, and Taylor</a>. Mathematical input from humans was limited to occasional high-level instructions from Tianyi: “Jacobian as a scheme sounds high priority,” “push [the] Mazur [theorem] to be done soon.” You can find excerpts of Claude’s thinking <a href="https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/9e431dff043da6538d99d6c2d231b670aa3da263.pdf">here</a>.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="codeBlock"><div class="PbWBnq_container"><div class="PbWBnq_code-container"><div role="presentation" style="position:relative;--scroll-area-corner-height:0px;--scroll-area-corner-width:0px" class="PbWBnq_ScrollArea" data-has-overflow-x="" data-overflow-x-end=""><div role="presentation" data-id="base-ui-_R_ijdrpbsnpfkq6btb_-viewport" tabindex="0" class="PbWBnq_Viewport base-ui-disable-scrollbar" style="overflow: scroll; --scroll-area-overflow-x-start: 0px; --scroll-area-overflow-x-end: 371px; --scroll-area-overflow-y-start: 0px; --scroll-area-overflow-y-end: 0px;" data-has-overflow-x="" data-overflow-x-end=""><div role="presentation" style="min-width:fit-content" class="PbWBnq_Content" data-has-overflow-x="" data-overflow-x-end=""><div><pre class="PbWBnq_code text-caption-1-mono" style="display:block;overflow-x:auto;background:transparent;color:#4d4d4c;padding:var(--spacing-24);margin:0"><code class="language-plaintext" style="white-space:pre"><span>“THE FLT root reads Proved on the site. Their failed efforts contributed ~7% of the non-boilerplate lines in the final proof.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">The effort succeeded when we switched to using <a id="base-ui-_R_3sldrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://prove2me.vercel.app/" rel="noopener noreferrer">Prove2Me</a>, an open collaborative platform for formalizing mathematics designed by Tianyi Peng and his collaborators at Columbia University. Prove2Me helped by:</p><ol class="PA4F1q_reading-column text-body-1-serif post-text"><li><strong>Maintaining a directed acyclic graph (DAG) of theorem statements</strong> that<strong> </strong>agents used to decide what proofs they should attempt next. This was particularly helpful for mitigating memory degradation and allowing multiple agents to work in parallel.</li><li><strong>Speeding up Lean compilation and minimizing resource consumption</strong> by separating theorem statements and proofs into different files, with the links between them maintained independently.</li><li><strong>Enabling search and reuse </strong>by maintaining a natural-language description of each theorem statement, resulting in a simpler proof path.</li></ol><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="DAG showing Claude formalizing sub-theorems on the way to FLT" loading="lazy" width="6800" height="3264" decoding="async" data-nimg="1" style="color:transparent" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/8b15d2015b54535f8fec.webp"><figcaption class="RMuY0W_root -LJkNq_container -LJkNq_container--default" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><div class="tOQyXa_caption-1 -LJkNq_text" data-component="Caption">Key milestones from the Prove2Me plan that Claude used to formalize Fermat’s Last Theorem. The finished proof was checked by Lean; it uses just Lean’s three standard axioms, and a <a id="base-ui-_R_3sodrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://github.com/leanprover/comparator" rel="noopener noreferrer">comparator</a> confirmed that the theorem’s statement matches Mathlib’s own statement of FLT.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="reducing-the-burden-of-formal-verification">Reducing the burden of formal verification</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">The speed with which we were able to produce this proof demonstrates that it is now possible to formalize large swaths of mathematics, which may both catch errors in the common body of mathematical proofs and reduce the burden of refereeing new work. After reviewing Claude’s Lean proof, Kevin Buzzard told us:</p><div class="PA4F1q_reading-column"><blockquote class="PA4F1q_blockquote post-text">If the automatic formalization of FLT is possible now, then we have taken a big step towards automatic formalization of the modern mathematical literature. Such autoformalization techniques will lead to new tools, rooting out errors in the current mathematical corpus and lightening the load of referees. The techniques will also enable us to rigorously check LLM-generated mathematics, which is currently typically an extremely costly human-led process.</blockquote></div><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Formalization is also a major factor in how humans can gain confidence in AI-generated mathematical results. As AI and AI-assisted mathematicians produce more (purported) proofs than ever before, AI-assisted formalization takes part of the load off human reviewers. We expect it will become common to produce a formalized proof alongside any write-up intended for a human reader. Although we do not think a formalized proof should replace a human-understandable exposition, it may be the only feasible way for the mathematical community to keep up with AI-generated contributions.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Writing Lean also seems to help Claude prove novel results. Many of our recent Claude-authored results have been formalized in parallel with their proofs, and Claude appears to use these partial proofs to independently check its hypotheses much like it writes numerical simulations to check that it’s on the right track.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Formalizing FLT was a token-intensive project, but it is also the largest Lean proof ever constructed. Anthropic researchers did a small experiment using three personal Claude Max plans to formalize applications of the Hardy-Littlewood Circle Method. Collaborating entirely through Prove2Me, the agents jointly completed a formalization of <a id="base-ui-_R_3sudrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vinogradov%27s_theorem" rel="noopener noreferrer">Vinogradov’s Three Primes Theorem</a> in just three days. We think with the right scaffold, collaborative formalization of major results with consumer AI subscriptions is achievable.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">To this end, <a href="/news/expanding-support-for-scientists">Anthropic</a> as well as <a id="base-ui-_R_7ovdrpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://openai.com/index/chatgpt-for-academic-researchers/" rel="noopener noreferrer">other labs</a> have recently expanded their support for external researchers—including mathematicians working on pure math and formalization—with free and discounted subscriptions and research credits. We also offer <a href="/news/ai-for-science-program">dedicated grants</a> for larger scientific projects, which could include formalizing other major theorems or improving Lean or Mathlib.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">With AI rapidly changing what it looks like to do math research, mathematicians—at Anthropic and elsewhere—<a id="base-ui-_R_3t0drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://leidendeclaration.ai/" rel="noopener noreferrer">are grappling with what that means for their work</a>. Formalization, however, is a place where we feel unambiguously good about the role of AI. As formalization becomes a more commonplace tool, we are hopeful that it will help maintain trust in the common body of mathematical knowledge.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="learn-more">Learn more</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">The full proof is available on <a id="base-ui-_R_3t2drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://github.com/anthropics/fermats-last-theorem" rel="noopener noreferrer">GitHub</a> along with a written walk-through of the proof.</p><h4 class="tOQyXa_headline-6 PA4F1q_reading-column post-subsection" data-component="Headline" id="recommended-expository-reading">Recommended expository reading</h4><ul class="PA4F1q_reading-column text-body-1-serif post-text"><li><a id="base-ui-_R_uj4drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://www.quantabooks.org/books/the-proof-in-the-code/" rel="noopener noreferrer"><em>The Proof in the Code</em></a> is a recent book about the history of the Lean theorem prover and the formalization of mathematics.</li><li><a id="base-ui-_R_ul4drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b0074rxx" rel="noopener noreferrer">The 1996 “Fermat’s Last Theorem” BBC documentary</a> has interviews with Wiles and other mathematicians involved in the proof, and is fondly remembered by some authors of this post.</li><li>For those with a mathematical background, a technical history of propositions-as-types (the underlying discipline of proof assistants such as Lean, Rocq, and Agda) can be found in <a id="base-ui-_R_v74drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/2699407" rel="noopener noreferrer"><em>Propositions as Types</em></a> by Philip Wadler.</li><li>Chen, S., Marwaha, K., Lu, X., Yuen, H., &amp; Peng, T.
