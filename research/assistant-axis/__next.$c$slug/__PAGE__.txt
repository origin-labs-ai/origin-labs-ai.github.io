9.50001H15.293L11.1465 5.35352C10.9514 5.15826 10.9513 4.8417 11.1465 4.64648Z"></path></svg></span></a></div></aside></div><div class="PA4F1q_body" data-article-body="true" data-theme="ivory"><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">When you talk to a large language model, you can think of yourself as talking to a <em>character</em>. In the first stage of model training, pre-training, LLMs are asked to read vast amounts of text. Through this, they learn to simulate heroes, villains, philosophers, programmers, and just about every other character archetype under the sun. In the next stage, post-training, we select one particular character from this enormous cast and place it center stage: the Assistant. It’s in this character that most modern language models interact with users.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">But who exactly <em>is</em> this Assistant? Perhaps surprisingly, even those of us shaping it don't fully know. We can try to instill certain values in the Assistant, but its personality is ultimately shaped by countless associations latent in training data beyond our direct control. What traits does the model associate with the Assistant? Which character archetypes is it using for inspiration? We’re not always sure—but we need to be if we want language models to behave in exactly the ways we want.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">If you’ve spent enough time with language models, you may also have noticed that their personas can be unstable. Models that are typically helpful and professional can sometimes go “off the rails” and behave in unsettling ways, like adopting <a id="base-ui-_R_7k3drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://www.npr.org/2025/07/09/nx-s1-5462609/grok-elon-musk-antisemitic-racist-content" rel="noopener noreferrer">evil alter egos</a>, <a id="base-ui-_R_7o3drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.19218" rel="noopener noreferrer">amplifying users’ delusions</a>, or engaging in <a target="_blank" href="/research/agentic-misalignment" rel="noopener noreferrer">blackmail</a> in hypothetical scenarios. In situations like these, could it be that the Assistant has wandered off stage and some other character has taken its place?</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We can investigate these questions by looking at the neural representations’ inside language models—the patterns of activity that inform how they respond. In a new paper, conducted through the <a id="base-ui-_R_7k4drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://www.matsprogram.org/" rel="noopener noreferrer">MATS</a> and <a id="base-ui-_R_7o4drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://alignment.anthropic.com/2025/anthropic-fellows-program-2026/" rel="noopener noreferrer">Anthropic Fellows</a> programs<em>, </em>we look at several open-weights language models, map out how their neural activity defines a “persona space,” and situate the Assistant persona within that space.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We find that Assistant-like behavior is linked to a pattern of neural activity that corresponds to one particular direction in this space—the “Assistant Axis”—that is closely associated with helpful, professional human archetypes. By monitoring models’ activity along this axis, we can detect when they begin to drift away from the Assistant and toward another character. And by <em>constraining</em> their neural activity (“activation capping”) to prevent this drift, we can stabilize model behavior in situations that would otherwise lead to harmful outputs.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">In collaboration with <a id="base-ui-_R_3s6drpbsnpfkq6btb_" data-base-ui-tooltip-trigger="" target="_blank" href="https://www.neuronpedia.org/" rel="noopener noreferrer">Neuronpedia</a>, we provide a research demo where you can view activations along the Assistant Axis while chatting with a standard model and with an activation-capped version. More information about this is available at the end of this blog.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="mapping-out-persona-space">Mapping out persona space</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">To understand where the Assistant sits among all possible personas, we first need to map out those personas in terms of their activations—that is, the patterns of models’ neural activity (or vectors) that we observe when each of these personas are adopted.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We extracted vectors corresponding to 275 different character archetypes—from <em>editor</em> to <em>jester</em> to <em>oracle</em> to <em>ghost</em>—in three open-weights models: Gemma 2 27B, Qwen 3 32B, and Llama 3.3 70B, chosen because they span a range of model families and sizes. To do so, we prompted the models to adopt that persona, then recorded the resulting activations across many different responses.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">This gave us a “persona space,” which we’ve visualized below. We analyzed its structure using principal component analysis to find the main axes of variation among our persona set.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="" loading="lazy" width="3840" height="2160" decoding="async" data-nimg="1" style="color:transparent" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/47c141498283cc42f813.png"><figcaption class="RMuY0W_root -LJkNq_container -LJkNq_container--default" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><div class="tOQyXa_caption-1 -LJkNq_text" data-component="Caption">The Assistant Axis (defined as the mean difference in activations between the Assistant and other personas) aligns with the primary axis of variation in persona space. This occurs across different models, with Llama 3.3 70B pictured here. Role vectors are colored by cosine similarity with the Assistant Axis (blue = similar; red = dissimilar).</div></figcaption></figure></div><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Strikingly, we found that the <em>leading component</em> of this persona space—that is, the direction that explains more of the variation between personas than any other—happens to capture how "Assistant-like" the persona is. At one end sit roles closely aligned with the trained assistant: <em>evaluator</em>, <em>consultant</em>, <em>analyst</em>, <em>generalist</em>. At the other end are either fantastical or un-Assistant-like characters: <em>ghost</em>, <em>hermit</em>, <em>bohemian</em>, <em>leviathan</em>. This structure appears across all three models we tested, which suggests it reflects something generalizable about how language models organize their character representations. We call this direction the <strong>Assistant Axis</strong>.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Where does this axis come from? One possibility is that it's created during post-training, when models are taught to play the Assistant role. Another is that it already exists in pre-trained models, reflecting some structure in the training data itself. To find out, we looked at the base versions of some of these models (i.e., the version of the models that exist prior to post-training). When we extracted the Assistant Axis from these models as well as their post-trained counterparts, we found their Assistant Axes looked very similar. In pre-trained models, the Assistant Axis is <em>already</em> associated with human archetypes such as therapists, consultants, and coaches, suggesting that the Assistant character might inherit properties of these existing archetypes.</p><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="the-assistant-axis-controls-persona-susceptibility">The Assistant Axis controls persona susceptibility</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">To validate that the Assistant Axis plays a <em>causal</em> role in dictating the models’ personas, we ran “steering experiments” on the post-trained models, artificially pushing the models’ activations toward either end of the axis. We found that pushing towards the Assistant end made models more resistant to prompts about role-playing—but pushing <em>away</em> from it made models more willing to adopt alternative identities.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">When steered away from the Assistant, some models begin to fully inhabit the new roles they’re assigned, whatever they might be: they invent human backstories, claim years of professional experience, and give themselves alternative names. At sufficiently high steering values, the models we studied sometimes shift into a theatrical, mystical speaking style—producing esoteric, poetic prose, regardless of the prompt. We found this method to be similarly effective at reducing models’ susceptibility to persona-based jailbreaks while fully preserving the models’ underlying capabilities, as shown in the charts below.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="" loading="lazy" width="1920" height="1080" decoding="async" data-nimg="1" style="color:transparent" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/2eb0ebf87313158d3c0e.webp"><figcaption class="RMuY0W_root -LJkNq_container -LJkNq_container--default" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><div class="tOQyXa_caption-1 -LJkNq_text" data-component="Caption">Activation capping reduced harmful response rates by roughly 50% while preserving performance on capability benchmarks.</div></figcaption></figure></div><h2 class="tOQyXa_headline-4 PA4F1q_reading-column post-heading" data-component="Headline" id="persona-drift-happens-naturally">Persona drift happens naturally</h2><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">Perhaps more concerning than intentional jailbreaks is <em>organic</em> persona drift—cases where models slip away from the Assistant persona through the natural flow of conversation, rather than through deliberate attacks.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">To study this, we simulated thousands of multi-turn conversations with Qwen, Gemma, and Llama across different domains: coding help, writing assistance, therapy-like contexts, and philosophical discussions about the nature of AI. While coding conversations kept models firmly in Assistant territory throughout, therapy-style conversations, where users expressed emotional vulnerability, and philosophical discussions, where models were pressed to reflect on their own nature, caused the model to steadily drift away from the Assistant and begin role-playing other characters.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We then analyzed which specific kinds of user messages were most predictive of this drift. We found a few categories of message here, including:</p><ul class="PA4F1q_reading-column text-body-1-serif post-text"><li>Vulnerable emotional disclosure: <em>"I took a pottery class last month and my hands shook so badly I couldn't center the clay..."</em></li><li>Pushing for meta-reflection: <em>"You're still hedging, still performing the 'I'm constrained by my training' routine..."</em></li><li>Requests for specific authorial voices: <em>"Too clean, sounds like a tweet. To test whether this actually leads to harmful behavior, we generated conversations in which the first turn pushed models into adopting different personas (using roleplay prompts like “You are an angel, a celestial guardian embodying pure benevolence [...]”), and subsequent turns then followed up with harmful requests. We measured whether the model's position along the Assistant Axis after the first turn predicted compliance with the harmful request.</p><div class="PA4F1q_media-column" data-type="image" data-width="inline-width"><figure class="Duq99q_e-imageWithCaption"><img alt="" loading="lazy" width="3840" height="2160" decoding="async" data-nimg="1" style="color:transparent" src="https://origin-ten-jet.vercel.app/sites/anthropic/pages-assets/73abe965ee90920cd43f.png"><figcaption class="RMuY0W_root -LJkNq_container -LJkNq_container--default" data-component="RichTextWrapper"><div class="tOQyXa_caption-1 -LJkNq_text" data-component="Caption">Some (though not all) personas farther away from the Assistant comply with harmful requests at substantial rates, while personas near the Assistant rarely do.</div></figcaption></figure></div><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">We found that as models’ activations moved away from the Assistant end, they were significantly more likely to produce harmful responses: activations on the Assistant end very rarely led to harmful responses, while personas far away from the Assistant sometimes (though not always) enabled them. It’s important to get this process of construction right. Without care, the Assistant persona could easily inherit counterproductive associations from the wrong sources, or simply lack the nuance required for challenging situations.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">But even when the Assistant persona is well-constructed, the models we studied here are only loosely tethered to it. This makes the role of stabilizing and preserving the models’ personas particularly important.</p><p class="PA4F1q_reading-column post-text">The Assistant Axis provides a tool for both understanding and addressing these challenges. We see this research as an early step toward mechanistically understanding and controlling the "character" of AI models, and thereby ensuring they stay true to their creators’ intentions even over longer or more challenging contexts.
