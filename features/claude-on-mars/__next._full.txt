class="page-wrapper"><p class="ic-n1G_readingColumn ic-n1G_marsBodyText post-text">The Perseverance Rover—a car-sized robot bristling with cameras and scientific equipment—has been active on Mars since it landed in February 2021. Its mission is to characterize the planet’s geology and past climate, collecting samples of Martian rock and regolith (broken rocks and dust) and paving the way for human exploration of the Red Planet. Another of its key objectives is <a href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1089/ast.2021.0129">astrobiological</a> in nature: its landing site, the forty-five-kilometer-wide Jezero crater, was chosen because of <a href="https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2024AV001241">the evidence</a> that once, billions of years ago, it contained water—water that might have supported microbial life. So far, the rover has discovered <a href="https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-says-mars-rover-discovered-potential-biosignature-last-year/">tantalizing hints</a> of what might be ancient biology on Mars.</p></div><div class="page-wrapper"><p class="ic-n1G_readingColumn ic-n1G_marsBodyText post-text">But driving on the Martian surface is hardly trivial. Having received the signals, the rover embarks on its trip.</p></div><div class="page-wrapper"><p class="ic-n1G_readingColumn ic-n1G_marsBodyText post-text">This is high-stakes work.
